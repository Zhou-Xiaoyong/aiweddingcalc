Five bridesmaids, one color, and a group text that goes silent the moment someone types the word budget. The dress is $215. The hem is $65. The shoes she was told to match run $95, and the bachelorette weekend is another $300 nobody has added up yet.

That gap between the sticker price and the actual outlay is where most bridal party friction starts, and it has nothing to do with whether anyone is being generous. It is a bookkeeping problem wearing an etiquette costume. So this page handles the arithmetic: bridesmaid dress cost line by line, then the four ways couples actually divide the bill. What a Bridesmaid Dress Costs in 2026

The working average is $150 to $250 per dress, which is the baseline our bridesmaid dress budget calculator runs on. That average hides a wide spread, and the spread tracks where you buy far more than what the dress is made of.

Where it is boughtTypical priceLead timeWhat the money buys
Department store, off the rack$60–$120Same dayA dress in whatever sizes are left, returnable
Online made-to-order$79–$1603–6 weeksPick color and silhouette, cut to order, final sale
Bridal salon house lines$100–$2308–12 weeksIn-store try-on, group order, in-house alterations
Designer bridesmaid collections$240–$40012–16 weeksFabric and construction, plus the label
Made to measure$400–$7006–10 weeksA pattern cut to her measurements

Two variables move the number as much as the label does. Fabric is one. Chiffon and crepe hang without much help and press easily; satin shows every crease and costs more to hem because the finish has to be redone. Timing is the other. A rush order inside the final six weeks adds $30 to $75 per dress at most retailers, and a party of six pays that fee six times.

Party size then multiplies everything. Four bridesmaids at the $200 line is $800 before tax. Seven is $1,400. Nothing about the dress changed between those two weddings.

The dress-only column stops at $468, and that is the number to negotiate over. Everything below the subtotal is optional in theory and expected in practice. Hair and makeup she pays for herself sits around $150 at most salons, the shower and bachelorette are social obligations with price tags attached, and none of it appears on the dress retailer's page.

Ask five women what they spent to stand in a wedding and you will get five answers between $500 and $1,200 with the same dress in all five. The variance is travel, the shower, and the bachelorette. Not the gown.

Bridesmaid Dress Cost and Who Pays: Four Splits

There is no surviving national rule. What exists is four structures couples use in practice, and each one has a number the couple can see before they commit. Figures below assume five bridesmaids and a $200 dress.

StructureWho pays whatCost to the coupleWhen it holds up
Everyone buys her own She covers dress, alterations, shoes $0 You picked from an affordable line and said the price out loud before anyone ordered
Couple covers the dress Couple: dress, tax, shipping. Her: alterations, shoes, jewelry About $1,140 You chose a specific style or color the party would not have picked alone
Couple covers everything dress-related Couple: dress through alterations and shoes About $2,340 Small party, or a budget that treats attire as part of the decor
Fifty-fifty on the dress Split the dress price, she keeps alterations and shoes About $500 You want to soften the ask without absorbing the whole line

The fourth option is the one couples forget exists. A 50/50 split is not in most etiquette books, and it works fine when the bride says it plainly: "I love this one, it's $215, I'd like to cover half." No one has to disclose a bank balance, and the couple's exposure stays under a sixth of the all-in version.

Apply whichever structure you choose to the entire party the same way. A split that covers four women and quietly leaves out the fifth is a friendship problem filed under budget, and it tends to surface at the bachelorette.

The Bridesmaid Dress Cost Nobody Budgets For

Five charges arrive after the order is placed and before the wedding. None of them appear on the product page.

  • Dye lots and reorders. Bridesmaid dresses are cut in dye lots. A second order placed six weeks after the first can land a shade off, and in group photographs it reads as a different color entirely. Put every dress in one transaction with one ship date, even if one woman needs extra time to pay.
  • Wrong size, no exchange. Bridesmaid sizing runs roughly two sizes smaller than street size, and special orders are almost always final sale. Measure bust, waist, and hips, then buy off the chart instead of the number on her jeans. A wrong size is a second dress, not a swap.
  • Rush fees. $30 to $75 per dress when the order goes in late. Six bridesmaids turns a $45 upcharge into $270.
  • Alterations priced by fabric. A hem runs $30 to $90 on a simple skirt and $65 to $110 on multi-layer chiffon. A bodice take-in is $40 to $80, strap work $15 to $40. Budget $75 to $200 per woman and you will rarely be surprised.
  • Pressing. $15 to $30 the week of the wedding, and worth it. A creased hem shows in every full-length photo.

Decide who absorbs these before the order goes in. The default in most parties is the bridesmaid, which is fine as long as she knows a $200 dress is a $290 commitment before she says yes.

When the Money Actually Leaves

The payment schedule matters as much as the total, because the charges land in five separate months and each one feels small on its own.

  1. Seven to nine months out: order placed. Most retailers and salons take 50% down, and this is the point of no return on color.
  2. Four to six months out: dresses arrive, balance due at pickup. This is when the party finds out how the color photographed in real light and whether the sizing chart told the truth.
  3. Eight to ten weeks out: first fitting, alterations deposit taken.
  4. Three to four weeks out: final fitting and the alterations balance, usually the largest single payment after the dress itself.
  5. Seven days out: steaming, shoes broken in, jewelry sorted. Small money, high stress.

One rule saves more goodwill than any script: the bride does not front the deposit. The moment she does, she becomes a collection agency for five friends in the four months before her own wedding, and the reimbursement texts arrive at the worst possible time. Take payment before you place the order, or have each bridesmaid order her own dress on the group ship date.

How to Cut the Number Without Looking Cheap

Almost all of the savings here come from logistics, not from buying a worse dress.

  • One retailer, one order, one ship date. Solves the dye lot risk and usually clears the free-shipping threshold, which saves $10 to $20 per dress.
  • Set color and length, not a single style. A party in the same shade with different necklines photographs better than six identical dresses, and each woman can buy at her own price point.
  • Skip the matching shoes. Tell them any nude or metallic heel they already own. Shoes barely register in ceremony photos and never in the album's full-length shots of the couple.
  • Use an independent tailor. Salon alterations carry a 20% to 30% markup. A local tailor who handles formalwear does the same hem for $45 to $70.
  • Shop the markdown windows. Online bridesmaid retailers clear last season's colors at 15% to 25% off in late November and again in January. A February wedding ordered in December hits one of those windows.
  • Resell afterward. A $200 dress in good condition brings $80 to $110 on resale sites, roughly 40% to 50% back. Tell the party before they buy; it changes how they feel about the price.

The one place not to cut is fit. A $120 dress that fits properly looks more expensive than a $300 dress that gaps at the bust, and the alteration line is what buys the fit.

What the Couple Should Put in the Budget

Bridal party attire usually runs 2% to 4% of the total wedding budget. Against the $34,200 national average (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study), that is $680 to $1,370, and it tells you something useful: covering five $200 dresses at about $1,140 fits inside the normal band, while covering dresses, alterations, and shoes at $2,340 does not.

That second number is close to 7% of the wedding, which puts it in direct competition with the florist or the videographer. If the choice comes down to those two, most couples get more from the video. Dresses are worn for eight hours. The video gets watched for decades.
