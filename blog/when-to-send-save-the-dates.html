The single most-asked paper question in wedding planning is also the one couples get wrong most often: when to send save the dates. Send too late and guests have already booked conflicting trips; send too early and details drift before the invitation lands. For a typical local wedding the answer is 6–8 months before the big day, but the right window shifts with your wedding type, destination, and season. With the average US wedding now running about $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study), the stationery line is small — usually $150–$600 — but the timing decisions it triggers affect travel, hotels, and your whole guest list. This guide gives you the exact window and the mechanics around it.

Why Save-the-Dates Exist (and When They Matter Most)

A save-the-date is a courtesy heads-up, not a formal invitation. Its only jobs are to reserve the date on a guest's calendar and to tell them the city, the weekend, and whether you've blocked hotel rooms. You send them when guests need lead time they wouldn't get from a standard invitation. That's almost always true for:

  • Destination or travel-heavy weddings — flights, time off, and hotels need months of lead time.
  • Holiday or three-day-weekend weddings — guests plan those weekends a year ahead.
  • Peak-season dates (June, September, October) when calendars fill fast.
  • Out-of-town-heavy guest lists — when most attendees need to book travel.

If everyone lives locally and your date is off-peak, save-the-dates are optional — but they still warm up the invite and reduce "I already booked that weekend" regrets.

The Core Rule: When to Send Save-the-Dates

Memorize one ladder, then adjust for type:

  • Local / standard wedding: 6–8 months before.
  • Popular weekend or peak season: 8–9 months before.
  • Destination wedding: 8–12 months before.
  • Holiday-weekend wedding: 9–12 months before.

Don't send a save-the-date until two things are locked: the date and the venue (even if it's just the city and state). A date change after save-the-dates go out means re-mailing everyone — awkward and costly.

Timing by Wedding Type

Break it down by how your guests travel:

  • Backyard / community-hall local wedding: 5–6 months is enough if most guests are within driving distance.
  • Hotel ballroom, same city: 6–8 months; guests may still need a hotel if they're from out of town.
  • Regional destination (another state): 8–10 months so guests can use points and book early fares.
  • International or resort destination: 10–12 months — passports, visas, and package deals all need the longest runway.

Holiday-Weekend & Peak-Season Weddings

If your wedding lands on Memorial Day, July 4th, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, or Christmas week, assume guests are deciding travel a year out. Send 9–12 months early and mention the room block on the card itself. The same logic applies to peak-season Saturdays in June, September, and October, when competing events fill calendars — 8–9 months keeps your date from getting bumped.

What to Include (and What to Skip)

Keep save-the-dates short and scannable:

  • Your names and the wedding date (spell out the day of the week).
  • City and state — enough for travel planning.
  • "Formal invitation to follow" so no one thinks this is the RSVP.
  • Hotel block link or code when you have one (a big help for travelers).
  • Website URL if it's live and has real info — not a placeholder.

Skip the venue's street address, dress code, and RSVP card — those belong on the invitation. Don't include registry info; etiquette says registry details go on the wedding website, not the save-the-date.

Addressing, Ordering & Mailing Mechanics

The logistics quietly eat more time than couples expect:

  • Finalize the guest list first. Everyone who gets a save-the-date gets an invitation — no exceptions. Trim before you print.
  • Order 10–15% extra for mistakes, address changes, and last-minute adds.
  • Address by hand or with printed labels — save-the-dates are more casual than invitations, so neat labels are fine.
  • Mail with forever stamps 1–2 weeks before your target window so they arrive on time.
  • Track wedding-party and family separately so no one's is lost in the batch.

Digital vs. Paper Save-the-Dates

Both are legitimate in 2026:

  • Paper: more personal, doubles as decor on a fridge, and feels official. Best for formal or older-skewing guest lists.
  • Digital (email or animated card): near-free, instant, and easy to track opens. Great for casual weddings, tight budgets, or very early sends.
  • Hybrid: mail paper to older relatives, email to friends. Just keep the send date consistent so no one feels slighted.

Digital saves real money — often the entire stationery difference between a $150 email blast and a $500 letterpress run. Weigh it against your overall plan with the wedding budget calculator.

How Much Save-the-Dates Cost

Budget by format:

  • Digital / email: $0–$40 for a design template.
  • Postcard (standard): $0.75–$2.00 each printed + postage (~$0.56–$0.69).
  • Card with envelope: $1.50–$4.00 each printed + postage.
  • Letterpress / foil premium: $4–$10+ each.

For 100 save-the-dates, that's roughly $130–$200 for postcards up to $600+ for premium cards. Model the whole stationery category — save-the-dates, invitations, RSVP, thank-yous — with the invitation cost calculator, and keep a cushion funded through the savings calculator.

Where Save-the-Dates Fit Your Invitation Timeline

The two mailings are linked but separate. Rule of thumb: invitations go out 6–8 weeks before the wedding (8–10 weeks for destination), with RSVPs due 3–4 weeks out. Save-the-dates simply book the date earlier. Count backward from your wedding:

  • Save-the-dates: wedding minus 6–12 months (by type above).
  • Invitations ordered: wedding minus 4–5 months (allow print + assembly time).
  • Invitations mailed: wedding minus 6–8 weeks.
  • RSVP deadline: wedding minus 3–4 weeks.

Never mail invitations before save-the-dates — guests who never got the heads-up may have already committed the weekend elsewhere.

Common Save-the-Date Mistakes

  • Sending before the venue is booked — date changes force a costly re-mail.
  • Too early for a local wedding — details drift and excitement fades; 6–8 months is plenty.
  • Forgetting the room block — travelers need it on the card, not later.
  • Inviting then un-inviting — only send to people you'll fully invite.
  • Putting registry info on the card — keep it on the website.
