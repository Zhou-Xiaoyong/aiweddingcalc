Two artists quoted for eight people, hair and makeup for all of them, come back at $3,462 for Saturday, November 28, 2026. The same two artists and the same eight faces on Saturday, November 14: $2,844. Thanksgiving weekend wedding hair and makeup runs $618 more for identical work.

Of that gap, $260 is the 15% peak-date premium and $255 is travel, a second artist, and an early call. The last $103 is the gratuity on both. Only the premium has anything to do with the holiday. The rest has to do with a clock.

Sunset on November 28 lands at 4:21 p.m. A 4:30 ceremony means portraits finish before 4:00, which means a bride dressed by 12:15, which means the first service starts at 6:45 in the morning. The team's van pulls in at 6:00 a.m. That is not a Thanksgiving problem. It is a schedule problem wearing a holiday date.

There is no holiday surcharge on this invoice. The premium is baked into each service price, which is why a November 28 quote and a November 14 quote look like a difference in artist quality rather than a difference in date.

The bridesmaid line is where that gets social. At $219 a head against $190 two weeks earlier, five friends pay $29 more per service, or $35 with the tip.

The three extras are honest and mostly non-negotiable. Travel moves because the artist is paying a holiday rate to whoever is watching her kids that Saturday. The second artist moves because her own rate moved. The $150 early call is the only line that exists because of the time of day, and it disappears if the ceremony starts at 5:30 instead of 4:30.

Why a 4:30 Ceremony Sets a 6:00 A.M. Call Time

Hair and makeup together take about 75 minutes a person when one artist handles both, which is how most independent teams work. Eight people is 600 minutes of labor. Two artists absorb it in four heads each, five hours of wall clock, with the bride last so her hair is the freshest thing in the room.

Here is the morning that produces, assuming 45 minutes of setup and a 4:30 ceremony.

Time Artist A Artist B
5:15 a.m. Van loaded the night before. Kits, two chairs, two ring lights, steamer, extension cords.
6:00 a.m. Arrival and setup. Nothing starts before the lights are up.
6:45 a.m. Mother of the bride Mother of the groom
8:00 a.m. Bridesmaid 1 Bridesmaid 3
9:15 a.m. Bridesmaid 2 Bridesmaid 4
10:30 a.m. Bride Bridesmaid 5

12:15 p.m. Dressed, jewelry, shoes. Someone finds the steamer again.
12:45 p.m. Portraits begin. Three hours and 36 minutes of usable light remain.

Two things there explain most beauty-budget arguments. The team works an eight-hour block, 6:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., and is paid for all of it even though the last 90 minutes are touch-ups and standing by with hairspray. And the mothers go first: a mother of the bride who hosted Thanksgiving dinner 48 hours earlier does not want a 10:30 chair.

At ten people the math breaks. Ten heads is 750 minutes, 6 hours 15 minutes for two artists, which a 4:30 ceremony cannot absorb without starting before 5:30. A third artist adds $350 to $450 and buys back two and a half hours. For ten or more, that is the best money on the invoice and the line couples cut first.

Move the ceremony to 3:00 and the first service lands at 5:15, arrival at 4:30, where teams that charge $75 per artist before 7:00 typically charge $100 before 5:00. Push it to 5:30 instead and the early call vanishes and you save $300, in exchange for zero daylight portraits.

Where the Peak-Date Premium Hides

Independent artists price November 28 the way they price a June Saturday, at 10% to 15% above their standard rate. Some write premium date into the contract. Most quote the higher number and let you assume it is normal.

One email settles it. Ask for a written quote on your date and a written quote for the same services on a non-holiday Saturday that month. The gap on eight people came to $260 here, which is 42% of the total difference between the two dates.

A trial is normally not premium-priced, because it happens on a quiet day, so the $160 stays $160. And gratuity is calculated on the premium too, which is why a 15% date premium adds more than 15% to the bottom line.

For the gratuity itself, 15% to 25% is the working range for stylists and artists. Our vendor tipping guide puts hair and makeup in the group that should always be a cash line, because a wedding morning is a salon visit that happens to be in your hotel suite.

The split follows convention: bridesmaids cover their own services, the couple covers the bride, the trial, and usually both mothers. Our guide to who pays for bridesmaid attire applies the same logic to dresses.

Which leaves the question that causes friction. Your date choice raised a bridesmaid's bill by $35. Do you pass it on? Absorb it. The premium exists because you picked the last Saturday in November, and charging five friends for a decision they did not make saves $175 against a decade of remembering it. If $175 is what stands between you and the budget, fewer services is the answer: hair only for the party drops that line from $1,314 to $726.

One contract term matters more than the price. Most beauty contracts allow service reductions up to 14 days out and charge in full after that. For November 28 the cutoff is November 14, nine days before Thanksgiving, in the middle of the week everyone is booking flights.

Booking Windows the Week of Thanksgiving

Thanksgiving week breaks the trial, not the wedding morning. A trial belongs three to six weeks out, which puts it in late October or early November. Book it after November 18 and you are asking for two hours during the busiest retail week of the salon's year.

  • November 21, Saturday: the last usable trial date. Any later and there is no time to change a foundation shade before the wedding.
  • November 24, Tuesday: fully booked with Thanksgiving-week blowouts. Nobody has two hours for a trial.
  • November 25, Wednesday: the busiest blowout day of the year, with chairs running from 7:00 a.m. to close and most shops shutting at 3:00 or 4:00.
  • November 26, Thursday: closed. Not reduced hours. Closed.
  • November 27, Friday: many shops open with two stylists instead of six, running retail promotions on products rather than services.
  • November 28, Saturday: three to six weddings per artist, all before noon.

A fresh blowout on Friday competes with every shopper in town and a staff half its normal size, and it is pointless anyway: a blowout done Friday afternoon is flat by Saturday noon.

Spray tans need a calendar line. A tan applied Wednesday, November 25 reads best on Friday and correct on Saturday. Thursday is closed everywhere.

Then the kit. Professional supply houses close and ship nothing from Wednesday through Friday, so a foundation shade that runs out gets replaced at a drugstore on Black Friday. Good teams restock two weeks ahead. Ask yours.

A 4:21 Sunset Changes the Makeup

A 4:30 ceremony on November 28 is a candlelight ceremony. The sun is nine minutes gone when anyone walks down the aisle, and the room is lit by whatever the venue owns plus whatever you rented.

That changes what the makeup should be, not just when it happens. A trial done at 11:00 a.m. in a salon with north-facing windows looks softer and brighter than the wedding will. Under warm 2700K candlelight and a photographer pushing ISO 3200, a natural look flattens from the cheekbones down. Ask for roughly 20% more cheek and lip pigment than the trial appears to need, and have the artist photograph the trial in a dim room.

Hair behaves differently too. Late November air is dry and cold, which means static on the way in and curls that drop faster than they would in June humidity. An updo or a braided style holds through a 4:30 ceremony and a 9:00 send-off. A blowout styled at 6:00 a.m. is a different shape by 2:00.

Two product notes. Skip SPF in the foundation, because titanium dioxide and zinc bounce flash back and turn faces pale white in every photo taken after dark. Pack blotting papers rather than powder, since a heated tent cycling between 55 and 72 degrees will make anyone shine by 7:00 p.m.

Where Thanksgiving Weekend Wedding Hair and Makeup Lands in a $34,200 Budget

The national average wedding costs $34,200 in 2026, per The Knot's Real Weddings Study. The couple's share here, $2,148, is 6.3% of that total, three times what most budget templates allocate for beauty.

Those templates are not wrong so much as narrow. Our 150-guest budget breakdown lists bridal hair and makeup at $450 to $600 including the trial, and bridesmaids at $150 to $200 a person. Both assume the bride is the only person on the couple's tab. Add two mothers, a second artist, travel, and a 6:00 a.m. call, and the couple's line is $2,148 while the party pays $1,314 directly.

The honest framing: the $500 line in a template is one chair. This wedding has eight. For your own number, add the bride's services, the trial, both mothers if you are hosting, travel, and the second artist, then run it against your guest count in the budget calculator. Beauty does not scale with guests, which is why it looks disproportionate at 88 and reasonable at 200.
