A wedding invitation lands in your coworker's inbox, the office starts buzzing, and suddenly you're asking the question nobody wants to say out loud: how much to spend on a coworker wedding gift. Too little feels cheap; too much feels like over-sharing. The good news is there's a comfortable norm, and it's smaller than most people fear. With the average US wedding now costing about $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study), couples aren't looking to coworkers to fund the day — they're looking for a kind gesture. This guide gives you the dollar ranges, the office-group etiquette, and the boundary rules so you can give with zero awkwardness.

The Short Answer

For a coworker, the typical 2026 range is:

  • Casual coworker (say hi in the hall): $20–$50, or nothing but a card.
  • Friendly coworker / same team: $50–$75.
  • Close work friend (you socialize outside work): $75–$100.
  • If you're invited to the wedding: $100–$150+ per person or couple.
  • Office group gift: $10–$25 per person toward a pooled $100–$300 total.

Notice the ceiling is far below what you'd give family. That's correct — a coworker gift is a courtesy, not a major financial commitment.

What Changes the Amount

Five factors shift you up or down within that range:

  • Closeness. Daily collaboration justifies more than a once-a-quarter meeting.
  • Attendance. Being at the wedding roughly doubles the appropriate amount — you're celebrating in person, not from the break room.
  • Office culture. In some workplaces a card signed by all is enough; in others a pooled gift is expected. Match the room.
  • Cost of living. In high-cost cities, the floor drifts up modestly ($50–$75 casual) — but the etiquette of "small is fine" still holds.
  • Your own means. Never give more than is comfortable. A $20 card is always acceptable; a strained $150 is not.

The Office Group Gift

The group gift is the cleanest solution for almost every workplace:

  • Opt-in, never mandatory. Send a quiet note; nobody should feel pressured.
  • Set a clear cap ($10–$25 per person) so the total is predictable.
  • One collector. A single point person gathers funds and presents the gift — avoids the "who gave what" weirdness.
  • One card, many signatures. A heartfelt group card carries as much weight as the cash.
  • Total sweet spot: $100–$300 buys a meaningful cash gift or a nice registry item.

If You're Invited to the Wedding

An invitation changes the math because you're now a guest, not just a colleague:

  • Aim for $100–$150 per person; a couple attending together can give $150–$200.
  • If you're also traveling or taking time off, a heartfelt $75–$100 is understood — your presence is the gift.
  • Buying off the couple's registry at the lower price points is an easy, can't-miss option.

If You're Not Invited

Hearing about the wedding without an invite removes any obligation:

  • A signed card from you (or the team) is a perfectly kind response.
  • A small desk-drop treat or a $20 gift is optional, not expected.
  • Don't feel guilty — gifts follow invitations, and no invitation means no expectation.

Cash vs. Physical Gift

For coworkers, cash or a gift card wins almost every time:

  • Practical: about 80% of couples prefer cash, and that's especially true for non-household givers.
  • Low-awkward: no risk of guessing their style or duplicating a registry item.
  • Easy logistics: a card in an envelope or a digital gift card ships to the desk with zero fuss.
  • Physical only if you know them well and spot a perfect, personal item under $75.

Registry vs. Off-Registry

If the coworker shared their registry at work, buying from it is the safest path — you know it's wanted. But a few rules:

  • Stick to lower price points ($25–$75) for a coworker; leave the $300 espresso machine to family.
  • Group a big item. If the team wants to buy the stand-mixer, pool for it rather than one person overspending.
  • Off-registry is fine too. A gift card or a small personal item works when no registry was shared.

Timing & Card Etiquette

  • Give before or at the wedding — a desk drop the week before is ideal; never weeks late.
  • Keep the card generic-warm ("Wishing you both every happiness") — save inside jokes for friends.
  • Don't out-give your manager awkwardly — the group gift neutralizes this entirely.
  • No receipts, no strings. A gift is a gift; don't track it.

Keeping It Inside Your Own Budget

Wedding season has a way of stacking coworker, friend, and family gifts in the same quarter. Hold the line by:

  • Capping coworker gifts at $50–$75 unless you're attending.
  • Defaulting to group gifts so your share stays tiny.
  • Using the wedding budget calculator to see gifting as one line inside your total plan.
  • Funding the gap with the savings calculator so a busy season doesn't blow your own wedding or honeymoon fund.

And if you're the one getting married, keep coworker gifts modest on your registry — a few low-price entries mean everyone at work can celebrate without strain.
