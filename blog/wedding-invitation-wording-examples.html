Eleven weeks out, a couple orders 120 invitations at $7.50 each. Three weeks after the envelopes hit mailboxes, the texts start: can we bring the kids, is the reception in the same building, do we pick a dinner or does the venue. None of that is a printing problem. It is a wording problem, and it costs a weekend of replies nobody budgeted for.

The card does more work than the paper stock. It tells guests who is hosting, what to wear, where to be, when to answer, and how many seats they hold. Get those five things onto the card in plain language and the follow-up calls mostly stop. What follows are wedding invitation wording examples you can hand to a printer as-is, all in American spelling and US date format so nothing needs rewriting before press.

The Six Lines Every Invitation Carries

Traditional invitations follow a fixed order, and guests read that order without thinking about it. Break it and the card looks off even when nobody can say why.

  1. Host line. Who is issuing the invitation. This is a fact about who is paying, not a ranking of who loves you more.
  2. Request line. Usually "request the pleasure of your company." Formal, understood, impossible to misread.
  3. Names and relationship phrase. "at the marriage of their daughter, Anna Whitmore, to Mr. Marcus Reed," or "at their wedding" when the couple hosts itself.
  4. Date and time, spelled out. "Saturday, the twelfth of June, two thousand twenty-six, at four o'clock in the afternoon." Numerals belong on the reply card.
  5. Venue and address. Building name, street address, city, and state. Skip the ZIP only if nobody will need to map the place.
  6. Reception line. "Dinner and dancing to follow," or "Reception immediately following at [venue]" when the party moves.

Dress code sits in the lower right corner as a short phrase. Nothing else belongs on the front: not the registry, not the website, not "no boxed gifts."

Wedding Invitation Wording Examples by Host

The host line is where most couples stall, usually because two households contributed money and nobody wants to imply otherwise. Pick the row that matches your situation and use the wording as written.

One set of parents hosting Mr. and Mrs. David Whitmore request the pleasure of your company at the marriage of their daughter, Anna Whitmore, to Mr. Marcus Reed Hosting parents first; the bride or groom identified as their child

Use "honor," not "honour," and hold that spelling across the invitation and inserts. If a parent holds a title you would use in writing (Dr., Judge, Reverend, military rank), spell it out instead of defaulting to Mr. and Mrs.

Wedding Invitation Wording Examples by Ceremony Type

The phrase after the names sets expectations about what guests will actually sit through.

  • Church or house of worship: "at the marriage of" for most Protestant ceremonies, "at the Nuptial Mass of" for a Catholic wedding with communion, and the officiant's name printed when a rabbi or pastor is central to the ceremony.
  • Civil or courthouse ceremony: "request the pleasure of your company at the marriage of," followed by the venue. "The Honorable Diane Foster officiating" is a good line if a judge is marrying you.
  • Destination wedding: "at their wedding celebration" rather than "at the marriage of," since the legal ceremony may happen at home. Travel details go on an insert, not the invitation.
  • Already married, now celebrating: "at a reception celebrating their marriage." Writing "at the marriage of" for a wedding that already happened is the most common error in this category, and guests notice.
  • Vow renewal: "at the renewal of their vows," with the anniversary year if you want it stated.

If your ceremony runs long or includes communion, note that on the wedding website. Guests plan around a start time, not a duration.

RSVP Card Wording That Gets Replies Back

Reply cards are where wording turns into money. Every unreturned card is a plate you either overpay for or add at the last minute, and the reply rates are lower than most couples expect: about 85% of local guests respond, 65% of out-of-town guests, and closer to 40% for international invites. On a 120-household list, that leaves 25 to 35 silent households you will be texting in the final month.

Three lines do most of the work:

  • The deadline, stated as a request: "The favor of a reply is requested by April 4, 2026." Set it three to four weeks before the wedding. Your venue wants a guaranteed count 7 to 10 days out, so a date two weeks before leaves no room to chase stragglers.
  • The seat count: "We have reserved 2 seats in your honor." Print the number per household instead of leaving a blank. It answers the plus-one and kids question without another sentence.
  • The action lines: "M ______________________" with "____ graciously accepts" and "____ regretfully declines" beneath it. The handwritten initial is what makes a card feel answered rather than assumed.

Meal choices belong on the reply card only when the kitchen needs them. Print three at most, and ask your caterer first: some venues charge a per-plate upcharge for a third entrée, which turns a fourth choice into a line item. "____ Chicken ____ Fish ____ Vegetarian" covers almost every guest at a plated dinner. A small "Please list any allergies" line saves a call to the kitchen.

Going digital? "Kindly reply at ourwedding.com/rsvp by April 4, 2026" works, and a QR card adds roughly $0.40 to $0.90 per invitation while cutting return postage. Our RSVP calculator applies those acceptance rates to your list so you know the headcount to plan for before the cards come back.

Dress Code Wording Guests Can Follow

Dress codes fail because guests read the weakest word in the phrase. "Optional" means "not required" to half the list, and "semiformal" means nothing to anyone.

Black tieTuxedo, floor-length gownEvening ballroom, hotel, country club
Black tie optionalAbout half arrive in tuxedos, the rest in dark suitsFormal venues where you would rather not exclude suit wearers
FormalSuit and tie, long or midi dressMost evening weddings that are not true black tie

Garden party / festiveSundress, linen suit, block heels for grassOutdoor venues, barns, tented receptions
Dressy casualNice jeans allowed, no shorts or flip-flopsBackyard or beach weddings

If you care about formality, print "Black tie" and accept that a few guests will underdress anyway. Print "Black tie optional" and expect a visible split in the room. Retire "semiformal" entirely: it lands anywhere between khakis and a cocktail dress.

Insert Cards: What Else Goes in the Envelope

Every insert adds weight, printing cost, and postage. On a 120-invitation run, one extra card typically adds $0.75 to $2.50 per invitation, plus enough weight to push the suite into a higher postage tier.

  • Reception card: required when the party moves to a second location. Venue, address, start time, and the "Adult reception" line if you need one.
  • Details card: the workhorse. Dress code, parking, shuttle times, weather notes, and the website URL on one card. It often replaces three others.
  • Accommodations card: hotel name, address, block code, cut-off date. Worth printing when more than a third of the list travels.
  • Welcome party or brunch card: only if you are hosting it. A separate card signals it is part of the weekend.
  • Registry card: never on the invitation itself. A separate enclosure or the wedding website is the accepted placement, and about 80% of couples now prefer cash or experience funds anyway.

The cleanest suite on a budget is one invitation, one reply card, and one details card with a QR code that leads to everything else. Three pieces instead of six saves roughly $150 to $300 on printing at 120 invitations. Square envelopes look better than they mail: they are non-machinable, so they carry a surcharge, and a loaded suite can run $1.25 to $1.50 per envelope. Weigh one assembled invitation at the post office before you buy 150 stamps.

Wording for Plus-Ones, Kids, and Headcount

Guest count is set by the envelope and the reply card, not by a conversation at the reception. Three lines keep it under control.

  • Name the adults on the outer envelope. "Mr. and Mrs. Reyes" means two seats. "and Guest" means three, so use it only when you truly do not know the name.
  • Print the seat count on the reply card. "We have reserved 2 seats in your honor" settles the children question gently, and it scales: a family of four gets a 4, a single friend gets a 1.
  • Put "Adult reception" on the reception card if the ceremony welcomes children but the evening does not. It states a fact about the party instead of a rule about their kids.

An inner envelope is the traditional place to list exactly who is invited, which is why stationers still sell them. Skip it and the seat-count line carries the same information for a fraction of the cost. Before you finalize, run the list through the wedding guest list calculator: at roughly $150 to $190 per guest in variable cost, twenty ambiguous plus-ones is a $3,000 to $3,800 decision, not an etiquette one.

What Wording Adds to the Bill

Invitations are priced per piece, and guests are not pieces. A 100-guest wedding usually needs only 65 to 75 invitations, since couples, families, and plus-ones share an envelope. Order 10 to 15% extra for spoilage and last-minute additions. Standard printing runs $3 to $10 per invitation; letterpress, foil, and engraving run $10 to $25 or more. The words cost nothing; the paper and postage do. Six Wording Mistakes That Trigger Phone Calls

  1. "No boxed gifts" printed on the invitation. It reads as a demand. Registry details go on an insert or the website.
  2. Mixing "honor" and "honour," or "marriage of" and "wedding of." Pick one register and hold it across the invitation and inserts. Mixed spelling looks like a template nobody proofread.
  3. Leaving off the city and state. Out-of-town guests will book a hotel in the wrong suburb. A venue name is not an address.
  4. An RSVP date that misses your caterer's deadline. If the venue needs a count 10 days out, a reply date 14 days out gives you four days to chase 30 households.
  5. "Black tie optional" on a wedding where you would be happy with khakis. The phrase pulls the room toward formal and leaves underdressed guests uncomfortable all night.
  6. "And guest" on every envelope. It is the fastest way to add 20 unplanned plates. Use it where you genuinely do not know the name, and pair it with a seat count.

Before you approve the proof, read the card out loud the way a guest will: name, date, place, what to wear, when to reply. Anything that takes a second pass becomes 120 copies of the problem.

If you change one thing from this page, put a hard reply date and a printed seat count on the RSVP card. Those two lines do more for your final headcount, and your catering invoice, than any design decision on the front.
