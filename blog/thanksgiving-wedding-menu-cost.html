A plated Thanksgiving dinner for 120 guests bills $16,519 once the 22% service charge and 8.5% sales tax land on the quote. The chicken plate the caterer sent in the first proposal, at $92 a head, bills $14,614. The holiday menu that sounds like the frugal choice prices $1,905 higher, and turkey is not the reason.

Couples order it anyway, and usually for a good reason. The date is set for the Saturday after Thanksgiving, half the family is already in town, and a turkey dinner with cornbread stuffing feels like the whole point. That part is free. What is not free is the package structure: a Thanksgiving menu means seven to nine sides instead of two, a carving station, and a kitchen crew working a holiday weekend rate.

Where the $104 Actually Goes

Caterers do not price protein. They price the number of dishes that have to leave the kitchen hot at the same time, and they price the people carrying them. Here is a realistic split of a $104 plated Thanksgiving plate in 2026.

Five percent of the plate is turkey. Twenty-one percent is the sides, and another twenty-seven is the kitchen labor that produces them, because nine dishes take three times the prep of three. A caterer who swaps filet for turkey frees up about $4 to $6 of food cost and still has to cook, hold, and plate nine components.

That is why asking for a cheaper protein moves the quote by $8 and asking for two sides instead of seven moves it by $16. If you want the Thanksgiving flavor without the package, ask for turkey, stuffing, one green, and one potato, served family-style. The Turkey Math That Sets Your Order

Once the menu is chosen, the next invoice question is how much bird to buy, and the answer surprises people who host Thanksgiving at home with a 20-pounder for twelve.

A whole turkey yields about 40% edible meat once the carcass, skin, and bones are gone. A 20-pound bird gives you roughly 8 pounds of meat, which is 18 portions at 7 ounces. Boneless breast yields closer to 65%, which is why caterers doing plated service buy breast and cooks doing buffet buy whole birds.

  • Plated dinner: 7 ounces cooked per guest. 120 guests need 53 pounds of cooked meat, or about 131 pounds of whole bird, which is seven 20-pounders.
  • Buffet: 1.3 to 1.5 pounds of raw bird per guest, because guests take more than a plated portion and come back for white meat. 120 buffet guests is 156 to 180 pounds raw.
  • Duo plate: cut the turkey portion to 4 ounces and the second protein carries the rest. This is the only version where two proteins cost less than one large one.

The carving station is a separate line and it is not food. Expect $450 to $900 for a chef, a second attendant, a board, and fuel, plus 90 minutes of payroll. It also duplicates work the kitchen already did, since the same turkey gets sliced before it goes out on a plated service. If your caterer offers a carving station as part of the package, ask what the plate costs without it.

Order against your guaranteed count, not your invited count. What the Holiday Weekend Adds to the Quote

Here is the line most couples miss until the revised proposal arrives. Catering is a labor business working a holiday, and labor gets repriced on a holiday weekend even when the food does not.

Staff on the Saturday after Thanksgiving are commonly billed at 1.25 times the standard rate, and on Thanksgiving Day itself at time and a half. Labor is about 39% of the plate once you add kitchen and service ($41 of the $104 above), so a 1.25 multiplier is roughly $10 a head and a 1.5 multiplier is closer to $20. On 120 guests, that is $1,230 to $2,460 that never appears on the menu page.

Three more holiday mechanics show up in contracts signed for that weekend:

  • Higher food and beverage minimums. Venues know the Saturday after Thanksgiving books itself. A property quoting a $12,000 food minimum for a September date often writes $15,000 for the holiday Saturday, and a full buyout appears in markets where the date is a destination draw. Compare minimums across dates before you fall for a site fee.
  • Five-hour staff minimums and overtime after hour five. Holiday crews are harder to split between events, so the four-hour reception you planned bills at five. A 7 p.m. dinner with a 6 p.m. cocktail hour and a 10 p.m. send-off lands at hour five before the last dance.
  • Dessert duplication. A pie station runs $5 to $7 a slice, plus $150 to $300 in rented pedestals and a server to cut and plate, and it does not replace the cake your family expects. A small cutting cake with two sheet cakes in the back runs about $4 a slice and covers everyone. Keep the pies, drop the second dessert course.

Weather moves the plate too. A November reception adds heaters to a tent ($1,400 to $1,800 for eight units), a coat check at $1 to $3 a guest, and hot beverages that a June bar never carries. None of that is catering, and all of it competes for the same dollars.

Three Ways to Bring the Plate Down

In order of money saved, not order of how fun they are.

  • Serve it buffet. $90.01 all in instead of $137.66, or $5,718 across 120 guests. You lose plated service and gain a line at the turkey. Ask for two carving stations for 120 people so the line moves, and staff the buffet with the servers you saved.
  • Go family-style with five platters instead of nine dishes. $116.48 all in, $2,541 under the plated turkey dinner. Passed platters read as generous, cost less in labor than individual plating, and let guests take the amount of stuffing they actually want.
  • Keep the turkey, cut the ceremony. Drop the carving station ($450 to $900), keep two sides plus stuffing, and move dessert to a single cutting cake with sheet cakes. That is $1,200 to $1,800 off without touching the guest count or the date.

What not to do: swap the turkey for chicken and keep the seven sides. You will save $5 a head on protein and still pay for nine dishes, which is the one combination that costs nearly as much as the holiday menu and tastes like a Tuesday.
