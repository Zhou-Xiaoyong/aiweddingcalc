Where you get married is one of the single biggest factors in what you'll pay — bigger, in many cases, than your guest count or your menu. A 150-person wedding that runs $50,000 in New York can come in at $28,000 in Mississippi for nearly identical service, simply because venue rent, vendor labor, and local demand are so different.

This guide breaks down the average wedding cost by state for 2026, covering all 50 states plus Washington, DC. For each state you'll find the average total cost, a realistic low-to-high cost range, and a note on what's driving prices. We've grouped the states into four tiers — Most Expensive, Above Average, Near Average, and Most Affordable — and within each tier organized them by US region so you can compare nearby options.

Why Wedding Costs Vary So Much by State

Before diving into the state-by-state numbers, it helps to understand why two weddings that look identical on paper can cost $20,000 apart. Four factors do most of the work:

1. Venue Availability

Desirable venues are a finite resource, and where supply is tight, prices climb. In dense Northeast metros and on islands like Hawaii and Martha's Vineyard, the number of large event venues within driving distance is small relative to the number of couples who want them. That scarcity lets venues charge premium minimums — often $15,000–$30,000 just for the room — and book 12–18 months out. In states with abundant barn, ranch, community-hall, and hotel-ballroom options, venue rent routinely runs $3,000–$8,000 instead.

2. Local Cost of Living

Vendors price their labor and overhead against the local economy. A photographer, florist, or caterer in San Francisco or Boston pays the same high rent, wages, and insurance as every other business there, and those costs are baked into your quote. The same vendor skillset in Memphis or Des Moines carries roughly half the overhead. Cost-of-living differences explain why the per-person catering rate in NYC ($95–$145) is nearly double the rate in the Midwest ($50–$80) for comparable menus.

3. Local Vendor Competition

More vendors competing for the same couples means more price pressure, more package deals, and more room to negotiate. Major metros like Chicago, Los Angeles, and Atlanta have hundreds of photographers, DJs, and florists — which actually keeps mid-tier pricing in check despite the high cost of living. Small markets and rural states may have only a handful of vetted vendors per category, so couples pay closer to the asking price and have fewer budget options to fall back on.

4. Tourism and Destination Demand

States that draw out-of-town visitors charge wedding premiums. Hawaii, Nevada (Las Vegas), coastal New England, Charleston, Nashville, and Napa all see heavy destination-wedding traffic, which pushes venue and vendor prices toward what an out-of-state couple will pay rather than what locals can afford. The upside is that these markets have a deep bench of wedding-ready vendors; the downside is the price floor is set by tourists.

Keep these four levers in mind as you read the state breakdowns — almost every state's cost profile is a combination of them.

Most Expensive States for Weddings ($40,000+)

Seven states average over $40,000 for a wedding in 2026. Six of them are clustered in the Northeast, and the seventh pair (California and Hawaii) are high-cost, high-demand markets on the West Coast and Pacific. If you live in one of these states, expect every line item — venue, catering, photography, even the officiant — to run 20–50% above the national average.

Northeast

  • New York — $50,000 ($38,000–$75,000): The most expensive state in the country. Manhattan and Brooklyn venue minimums routinely clear $20,000, the Hamptons and Hudson Valley command destination premiums, and a deep high-end vendor market means there's always someone willing to charge more. Upstate New York is dramatically cheaper, but the state average is pulled up by NYC metro volume.
  • New Jersey — $47,000 ($35,000–$70,000): Dense NYC commuter suburbs with large Italian and South Asian wedding traditions mean big guest lists (often 200+) and country-club and banquet-hall venues with high food-and-beverage minimums. The northern half of the state tracks NYC pricing; South Jersey is meaningfully cheaper.
  • Rhode Island — $46,000 ($35,000–$65,000): Tiny state, big prices. Newport's Gilded Age mansions and coastal resorts make Rhode Island a destination-wedding hotspot, and peak-season Saturday dates at waterfront venues book a year out and carry $15,000+ minimums.
  • Massachusetts — $45,000 ($34,000–$65,000): Boston metro drives the average, with hotel and museum venues charging urban premiums and a highly educated, high-income vendor base. The Cape and islands (Martha's Vineyard, Nantucket) push the upper end of the range even higher in summer.
  • Connecticut — $44,500 ($33,000–$63,000): Part of the NYC commuter belt, Connecticut has a deep country-club and estate-venue market and some of the highest household incomes in the US, which vendors price against. Fairfield County tracks NYC pricing; the eastern half of the state is somewhat more affordable.

West

  • California — $43,000 ($32,000–$70,000): The San Francisco Bay Area, Los Angeles, and San Diego all carry top-tier venue and vendor costs, and Napa/Sonoma wine-country weddings command destination premiums. The Central Valley and far north are cheaper, but most California weddings happen in the expensive coastal metros. The range is wide because California spans everything from a $25K courthouse-adjacent celebration to a $150K vineyard extravaganza.
  • Hawaii — $42,000 ($32,000–$65,000): A near-pure destination market. Almost everything — flowers, rentals, even some food — has to be shipped in, labor costs are high, and resorts charge tourist-level pricing. The result is one of the highest averages in the country, even though local couples often host smaller guest lists.

Above Average States ($35,000–$40,000)

Twelve states plus DC land in the above-average tier. These are predominantly major-metro states (DC, Maryland, Virginia, Illinois, Washington) and high-demand destination markets (Colorado, Nevada, Vermont). Couples here pay a premium, but it's a softer one than in the top tier — and most of these states have affordable pockets if you're willing to leave the metro core.

Northeast

  • Pennsylvania — $36,500 ($27,000–$52,000): Philadelphia drives the high end with hotel and historic-venue pricing, while Pittsburgh and the central counties run considerably lower. Amish-country venues around Lancaster offer scenic, affordable alternatives.
  • Vermont — $35,000 ($26,000–$50,000): A destination market for barn, farm, and ski-resort weddings drawing couples from Boston and New York. Limited venue inventory and short peak seasons (June–October) keep Saturday premiums high.
  • New Hampshire — $35,000 ($26,000–$48,000): Absorbs Boston spillover in the southern tier, with mountain and lake venues popular for summer and fall weddings. No sales tax helps slightly on rentals and favors.

Midwest

  • Illinois — $38,000 ($28,000–$55,000): Chicago is one of the most expensive wedding markets between the coasts, with hotel-ballroom and loft-venue minimums pushing the average up. Downstate Illinois (Springfield, Champaign) runs $10,000–$15,000 cheaper for a comparable wedding.

South

  • Washington, DC — $39,500 ($30,000–$58,000): A compact, high-income urban market with premium hotel and museum venues and a vendor base that prices against federal-salary and lobbyist clientele. The highest average outside the top-tier states.
  • Maryland — $39,000 ($29,000–$55,000): DC suburbs (Bethesda, Annapolis) carry DC-adjacent pricing, and Annapolis waterfront venues command a premium. Western Maryland and the Eastern Shore offer more affordable options.
  • Virginia — $38,500 ($28,000–$55,000): Northern Virginia tracks DC pricing, while Richmond, Charlottesville, and the historic plantation venues of the Tidewater region set their own (still above-average) rates. Rural Virginia is much cheaper.
  • Delaware — $35,500 ($26,000–$50,000): A small state where beach-town venues (Rehoboth, Bethany, Lewes) drive peak-season premiums, while northern Delaware (Wilmington area) sits closer to the Philadelphia market.

West

  • Washington — $37,500 ($28,000–$55,000): Seattle and the Eastside carry the high end, with winery venues in Woodinville and Yakima adding destination appeal. Spokane and the rest of eastern Washington are significantly more affordable.
  • Colorado — $37,000 ($27,000–$55,000): Denver, Boulder, and mountain resorts (Aspen, Vail, Telluride) make Colorado a destination-wedding state, with ranch and mountain venues charging peak-season premiums. The Front Range has more affordable barn and brewery venues.
  • Nevada — $36,000 ($25,000–$60,000): Las Vegas dominates and creates an unusually wide range — a $400 chapel elopement sits at one end and a $60K Strip resort wedding at the other. Reno and the rest of the state run closer to $28,000–$32,000.
  • Oregon — $35,500 ($26,000–$50,000): Portland metro and Willamette Valley winery venues set the average, with a strong farm-to-table catering culture that isn't cheap but is high quality. Southern and eastern Oregon are more affordable.

Near Average States ($30,000–$35,000)

Fourteen states sit right around the national average. This is the sweet spot for many couples — enough venue and vendor options to have real choices, but without the coastal premium. Most of these states have one or two major metros that pull the average up while the rest of the state stays affordable.

Northeast

  • Maine — $31,000 ($23,000–$45,000): Coastal Maine (Portland, Bar Harbor, Camden) is a summer destination market with premium pricing, but the season is short and the rest of the state is quite affordable. Book a September date for good weather and lower rates.

Midwest

  • Minnesota — $34,500 ($25,000–$48,000): Minneapolis–St. Paul is the price driver, with a strong vendor scene and venue options from loft spaces to lake lodges. Greater Minnesota runs $8,000–$12,000 cheaper.
  • Ohio — $32,000 ($23,000–$45,000): Cleveland, Columbus, and Cincinnati each have their own vendor markets at similar price points, with barn and estate venues popular across the state. A solid, affordable mid-tier market.
  • Wisconsin — $31,500 ($23,000–$45,000): Milwaukee and Madison anchor the market, with barn and lakeside venues popular statewide. A short outdoor season keeps summer Saturdays in demand.
  • Michigan — $31,500 ($23,000–$46,000): Detroit and Grand Rapids metros set the average, with lakefront and winery venues in Traverse City drawing destination traffic in summer.

South

  • Texas — $33,500 ($24,000–$55,000): A huge state with a wide range. Austin has the highest venue and vendor prices, Dallas and Houston sit a step below, and San Antonio, El Paso, and rural Texas run well under $30,000.
  • Florida — $33,000 ($24,000–$55,000): Miami and the Keys push the high end with destination beach weddings, while Orlando, Tampa, and Jacksonville sit near the national average. Panhandle and inland Florida are the most affordable pockets.
  • Georgia — $33,000 ($24,000–$48,000): Atlanta metro drives the average, with a deep vendor pool that actually keeps mid-tier pricing competitive. Savannah adds a destination premium; south Georgia is quite affordable.
  • North Carolina — $32,500 ($24,000–$47,000): Charlotte and Raleigh–Durham are the main metros, and Asheville is a mountain destination market with premium pricing. The coast (Wilmington, Outer Banks) also draws destination weddings.
  • Tennessee — $31,000 ($23,000–$48,000): Nashville is the price outlier — a booming destination-wedding market with venue premiums — while Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga run well below the state average.
  • South Carolina — $31,000 ($22,000–$48,000): Charleston is one of the South's premier destination-wedding cities and carries the highest prices in the state; Columbia, Greenville, and the rest of the state are much more affordable.

West

  • Arizona — $34,000 ($25,000–$50,000): Phoenix and Scottsdale resort weddings drive the average, with desert and golf-course venues popular for winter and spring destination weddings. Tucson and Flagstaff run lower.
  • Utah — $32,500 ($23,000–$45,000): The Salt Lake City–Provo market has a distinctive cost profile: very large family guest lists (often 200+) push the total up even though per-person costs are moderate. The per-guest rate is actually among the lowest in the country.
  • Alaska — $30,500 ($22,000–$45,000): A small vendor pool and shipping costs for rentals and flowers push prices above what you'd expect for the population, but a short outdoor season and modest guest lists keep the average near the national mark.

Most Affordable States ($25,000–$30,000)

Eighteen states make up the most affordable tier. These are concentrated in the South, the Great Plains, and the Mountain West, where a lower cost of living, smaller typical guest lists, and less tourism-driven venue demand combine to keep prices down. A wedding in any of these states can easily run $15,000–$20,000 less than the same wedding in New York or California — without sacrificing quality.

Midwest

  • Indiana — $29,500 ($21,000–$42,000): Indianapolis and Bloomington anchor an affordable market with lots of barn, church, and community-venue options. Per-person catering runs well below the national average.
  • Iowa — $28,500 ($20,000–$40,000): Des Moines, Cedar Rapids, and Iowa City offer a solid vendor scene at low prices, with rural venues even cheaper. A genuinely affordable place to host a 150-person wedding.
  • Kansas — $28,500 ($20,000–$40,000): Kansas City suburbs (Overland Park) sit at the higher end; Wichita and the rest of the state run lower. Limited destination demand keeps venue premiums minimal.
  • Missouri — $29,500 ($21,000–$42,000): St. Louis and Kansas City are the two main metros, each with competitive vendor markets. Springfield and Columbia run a bit cheaper.
  • Nebraska — $28,000 ($20,000–$39,000): Omaha and Lincoln have enough vendors to keep prices competitive, and the rest of the state is very affordable. Low venue minimums are the norm.
  • North Dakota — $27,000 ($19,000–$38,000): The least expensive state in the country. A small population means small guest lists and modest venues, and vendor prices track a low cost of living.
  • South Dakota — $27,500 ($19,000–$39,000): Similar story to North Dakota, with Sioux Falls and Rapid City as the main markets. The Black Hills draw some destination weddings but at modest price points.

South

  • Alabama — $29,500 ($21,000–$42,000): Birmingham and Huntsville are the main metros, with low venue and catering costs. The Gulf Coast (Mobile, Gulf Shores) adds a modest beach premium.
  • Arkansas — $29,000 ($21,000–$40,000): Little Rock and Fayetteville anchor an affordable market; the Ozarks and Hot Springs draw some destination weddings without pushing prices into the stratosphere.
  • Kentucky — $29,000 ($21,000–$42,000): Louisville and Lexington have a distinctive horse-country venue scene (barns, bourbon distilleries) at reasonable prices. The rest of the state is very affordable.
  • Louisiana — $29,500 ($21,000–$45,000): New Orleans is the price outlier, with destination weddings and a vibrant vendor scene pushing the high end up. Baton Rouge, Lafayette, and the rest of the state run well below $28,000.
  • Mississippi — $27,500 ($19,000–$38,000): The second-cheapest state. Low cost of living, small guest lists, and minimal destination demand keep prices at the floor of the national range. Jackson and the Gulf Coast are the main markets.
  • Oklahoma — $28,500 ($20,000–$40,000): Oklahoma City and Tulsa are the two metros, both affordable, with a growing barn and ranch venue scene. Per-person catering is among the cheapest in the country.
  • West Virginia — $28,000 ($20,000–$40,000): A low-cost-of-living state with scenic mountain and lodge venues that draw some regional destination traffic without driving up prices significantly.

West

  • Idaho — $29,000 ($21,000–$42,000): Boise is growing fast and pulling prices up slightly, but most of the state remains very affordable, with ranch, mountain, and lakeside venues at low rates.
  • Montana — $29,500 ($21,000–$45,000): A split market. Local weddings in Billings, Missoula, and Bozeman are affordable, but destination ranch weddings (Big Sky, Whitefish) push the upper end of the range up considerably.
  • New Mexico — $29,000 ($21,000–$42,000): Albuquerque and Santa Fe are the main markets. Santa Fe carries a modest destination premium for its resort and historic-venue scene; the rest of the state is very affordable.
  • Wyoming — $28,500 ($20,000–$42,000): The smallest population of any state means small guest lists and limited vendor options, but also low venue minimums. Jackson Hole is the destination exception with much higher pricing.

How to Save on a Wedding in an Expensive State

If you live in a high-cost state, you don't have to move your wedding to afford it. The same levers that lower costs anywhere — guest count, day of week, service style — just deliver bigger dollar savings when the baseline is high. Here are the most effective strategies for expensive states specifically:

1. Cross the State Line

Venue and vendor pricing can drop 20–40% within a 60–90 minute drive of a major metro, often across a state line. A New York couple can save $10,000+ by hosting in the Hudson Valley or Connecticut; a DC couple can cut costs sharply by moving to Virginia wine country or rural Maryland. Most guests will drive an hour for a wedding.

2. Pick an Off-Peak Date

In destination and high-cost markets (Newport, Napa, Charleston, Hawaii), Saturday nights in peak season carry the biggest premiums. A Friday, Sunday, or a Saturday in the shoulder season (November–March in most of the country) can save 15–30% on venue minimums and often unlocks vendor discounts too.

3. Shrink the Guest List

This is the single biggest lever, and it's even more powerful in expensive states where per-person catering runs $100–$145 instead of $60–$85. Cutting 30 guests in New York saves roughly $3,000–$4,500 on catering alone, plus another $1,500+ on bar, cake, rentals, and favors. Build your list in tiers so you can trim strategically.

4. Choose a Non-Traditional Venue

Hotel ballrooms and dedicated wedding venues in expensive markets carry the highest minimums. Restaurants, breweries, art galleries, community halls, and public parks often charge a fraction of the room fee — and many let you bring in outside catering, which opens up cheaper per-person options.

5. Book Vendors From a Cheaper Nearby Market

Photographers, DJs, and florists will often travel 1–2 hours for a travel fee of $100–$400 — which is far less than the price gap between a Boston vendor and a Providence or Worcester vendor. Just confirm they're licensed and insured for your venue's location.

6. Skip the Open Bar

A full open bar runs $45–$85+ per person in expensive states. A beer-and-wine bar ($20–$35 per person) plus one or two signature cocktails covers what most guests actually drink and saves 40–60% on the bar line item — easily $2,000–$4,000 on a 150-guest wedding.

7. Get Married in City Parks or Public Spaces

Many cities operate historic sites, botanical gardens, and waterfront pavilions that rent for a few hundred to a few thousand dollars — a tenth of what a private venue charges. Permit requirements vary, so book early and confirm guest-count and catering restrictions.

8. Host a Destination Wedding in an Affordable State

If you're in a top-tier state and most of your guests would travel anyway, consider hosting in an affordable destination. A 150-guest wedding in New Orleans, Asheville, or Savannah can run $10,000–$15,000 less than the same wedding in New York — and the travel costs for guests are often comparable to what they'd spend getting to a domestic wedding anyway.
