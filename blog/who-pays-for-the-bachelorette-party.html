The short answer to who pays for the bachelorette party is the group, not the bride. In 2026 that still means the bridesmaids and the bride's friends cover the weekend while she arrives with a bag and no bill. What trips people up is the arithmetic hiding inside that sentence. On an eight-person weekend with $4,200 of shared expenses, a bride who pays nothing adds $75 to every other attendee's share, and usually nobody agreed to that number out loud.

Most bachelorette arguments are not really about etiquette. They are about a $600 charge that appeared in a group chat three weeks before the trip, with no context and no way to say no without feeling cheap. This guide puts the numbers on the table first, then the wording.

Who Pays for the Bachelorette Party: The 2026 Default

The working default across most of the US: attendees split the shared costs evenly, and the bride's share is absorbed by the group. Her airfare is a separate question, and it is the one place where norms have genuinely loosened.

Here is what the three common structures cost on a three-night domestic weekend. Shared costs of $4,200 for eight people cover the house, groceries, two dinners, one activity, drinks, and local transport. Airfare is $380 per person and always paid individually.

Bridesmaids split, bride free7 paying attendees, $75 each$600$980

The third option is generous and rare. It only works when the host volunteers before anything is booked, because a maid of honor who discovers in month two that she is funding a weekend will resent every dinner on it.

One more arrangement deserves a mention because it is increasingly common: a parent hosts, either by covering the house or by writing a check for the bride's portion. If a mother or an aunt offers, take the offer and say thank you. Do not turn it into a negotiation about who else should contribute.

What a Free Bride Actually Costs Each Attendee

The percent matters less than the absolute number, so run both. On a $905 weekend the free-bride convention adds 8.3% to every other attendee. On a longer trip it grows faster than the trip does, because the bride's share is a fixed fraction of a bigger base.

Take a four-night trip to Mexico with eight women at $1,400 per person. Shared costs of $5,600 divide to $700 each, or $800 each across seven once the bride stops paying. That is a $100 difference, and $100 is the point where people start quietly deciding not to come next time.

Where the line sits, roughly:

  • Under $400 per person: the free bride is a non-issue. Nobody notices and nobody should be asked to.
  • $400 to $900: fine, but state it in the first message. This is the range where silence turns into resentment.
  • Over $900: the free ride is a real gift, and it should be framed as one. Many groups switch to "everyone pays their share, and we cover her dinner and drinks."

The bride is not free either, which is the part the group chat forgets. She is typically out $300 to $600 of her own money on the trip: her outfit, a round of drinks, small thank-you gifts for each attendee at $30 to $60 apiece. A bride who pays her own share and then buys the first night's dinner has solved the fairness problem without a single awkward message, and that costs her far less than the group thinks.

Four Split Structures, and Which One Fits

Even split, everyone included. Cleanest, and the one to default to when the trip exceeds $900 per person. One number, one total, no sub-ledger. The bride pays like everyone else and the group spends the goodwill on a nicer dinner.

Bridesmaids split, bride free. The traditional model, and still the norm for local bachelorettes where the share is $150 to $250. It works because the amount is small enough that nobody audits it.

Bridesmaids absorb the bride, friends pay flat. This is the structure that causes trouble, and the math shows why. Four bridesmaids, three outside friends, one bride, $4,200 shared. The three friends pay $525 each, leaving $2,625 for four bridesmaids at $656 each. The bridesmaids pay $131 more than the friends for the same house and the same meals, and one of them will notice.

A host covers the bride. A parent, an aunt, or a maid of honor with the means and the appetite for it. Clean, but only if it is offered before deposits go down and never announced as a sacrifice.

Whatever you pick, write it in the same message as the dates. "Three nights in Scottsdale, about $600 each including the house, everybody pays their own flight" is a complete brief. "Let's do Scottsdale!" is not, and it is how the $600 surprise gets born.

Who Pays for the Bachelorette Party When It's a Destination Weekend

Airfare is individual. Every group I have seen handle this well treats flights as the attendee's own problem and pools only the shared costs, because a flight price depends on where you fly from and nobody should subsidize a hub city.

The house is where the money gets messy. Split by head, not by bedroom, unless the bedrooms are genuinely unequal and someone volunteers for the sofa bed at a discount. A four-bedroom that sleeps ten means two people are on a pull-out for three nights, and charging them the same as the king suite is how you lose a friendship over a mattress.

The quoted nightly rate is also not the number you pay. A $1,800 listing across three nights lands closer to $2,300 at checkout once the cleaning fee ($150 to $400), the platform service fee (roughly 14% on most short-term rental sites), and local occupancy tax (10% to 15% in cities that tax short-term rentals) are added. Budget the checkout total, not the listing total, and re-split the group after you see it.

Watch the occupancy cap too. Plenty of listings advertise space for ten and cap the booking at eight because of parking, septic, or a local ordinance. A ten-person group then needs two units, and the second unit can add $700 to $1,200 to the weekend. Ask the host what the permit allows before you invite the ninth person.

The Charges That Land After the Split

Every group budget I have seen comes in low, and it comes in low on the same four lines.

  • Restaurant auto-gratuity. Parties of six or more get an automatic 18% to 20% gratuity at most restaurants in Miami, New York, Los Angeles, and resort towns, and it is printed in small type at the bottom of the menu. Eight people at $95 a head is $760, plus 20% is $912, plus sales tax. The group that budgeted $760 per dinner is $200 short.
  • Private room deposits. $250 to $500, applied to the bill only if you hit the food and beverage minimum. Cancel and it is gone. Read the minimum before you book a room for a party of eight that might spend $700.
  • Transport with a minimum. A party bus or a pontoon runs a three-hour minimum at $700 to $1,200, plus fuel, plus a 15% to 20% driver gratuity on the total. That gratuity is often quoted as "optional" and is not treated as optional by anyone driving eight women at 1 a.m.
  • Decor and matching merchandise. Sashes, balloons, tumblers, the banner that says her name. Budget $15 to $25 per person, which is $120 to $200 for eight, and it is the line item that never appears in anyone's estimate.

The last hidden cost is float. Whoever books the house carries $4,000 to $5,000 on a personal credit card for weeks. Collect before you book, not after the trip. And if the group insists on instant transfers, do the math: the Venmo instant fee is 1.75% with a $25 cap, so one transfer of $4,200 costs $25 while seven transfers of $600 cost about $73.50. Have everyone send to one collector and let that person move the money once.

When One Person Can't Afford It

Someone always can, and the group usually finds out at the worst possible moment. The fix is structural: nobody should be asked to commit to a bachelorette over $500 without seeing the number first. Put it in the invitation message, before the yes.

Declining is allowed and should be treated as normal. The etiquette that has settled in: the person who skips still sends a card and chips $50 to $100 toward whatever the group is doing for the bride. No explanation required, no group guilt, no follow-up message asking whether she is sure.

If the whole group is stretched, shrink the format rather than the headcount. Three options with real numbers:

  • Dinner and one hotel night. $180 to $250 per person, still feels like an occasion, still produces the photos.
  • Spa day and lunch. $150 to $200 per person, no flights, no house, no cleaning fee.
  • Two nights within driving distance. $320 to $400 per person, and it keeps the weekend shape that a single dinner cannot.

If one person is the only one who cannot swing the destination trip, do not downgrade the whole plan for her and do not offer to cover her publicly. Let her opt out gracefully, and if you can quietly cover part of it, do that in a direct message with no group thread attached.

What the Maid of Honor Actually Says

Wording does most of the work here. Three messages cover 90% of the situations.

The opening ask, with the number attached: "Thinking Scottsdale, three nights in late April. It works out to about $600 each for the house, food, and the boat day, plus your own flight. No pressure and no rush, just want you to have the number before you say yes."

The bride's share question, asked of the group and not of one person: "For her share, I was thinking we split it seven ways, which is about $75 more each. If anyone would rather do even split, say so now and we will do that instead, no hard feelings."

When someone says it is too much: "Totally fine, we will miss you. I will send photos and we will do dinner just us before the wedding." Then stop. Do not problem-solve in the thread.

Notice what none of these do: apologize for money existing, or turn the weekend into a referendum on friendship. A bachelorette is a party with a price tag. Treating the price tag as ordinary information is what keeps the party fun.

Where the Bachelorette Fits in the Wedding Budget

The bachelorette is almost never part of the couple's wedding budget. The $34,200 average US wedding (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study) covers the venue, catering, photography, attire, and the reception, and it does not include a weekend in Nashville that the bridal party funds separately.

What does belong in the couple's budget: the bride's own flight and share, her outfit for the trip ($80 to $200), and any thank-you gifts she gives the attendees. If the couple is hosting the whole thing, move it into the events column and plan it like a small party, which is exactly what the event budget calculator is built for.

For a bridesmaid, the bachelorette is one line in a much larger bill. Dress-related spending lands around $470. Add hair and makeup, a shower gift, and a local bachelorette share and the total is closer to $870, which is the figure our bridesmaid dress budget calculator uses. Swap the local night for a $905 destination weekend and the same woman is past $1,600 before she buys a wedding gift.

That last number is why the group gift matters. Thirty people chipping $20 beats eight people chipping $150, and it keeps the wedding party out of debt before the wedding. Size the contribution in the wedding party gift calculator before anyone volunteers a number in the chat.

If the bride is covering the dresses, the proposal boxes, and the bachelorette herself, that is real money on top of the wedding. Map all of it against the full picture in the wedding budget calculator rather than discovering it across four separate credit card statements.

The Number to Plan Around

Three numbers cover nearly every bachelorette in the US right now. A local one-night is $150 to $250 per person. A domestic three-night weekend is $600 shared plus your flight, so roughly $900 all in. A four-night international trip is $1,400 and up.

Pick the structure before the dates, not after. Even split if the trip clears $900. Bridesmaids absorb the bride if it stays under $400. And if a parent offers to host, accept it in one sentence and move on.

Then put the number in the first message you send, with the dates and the destination, and let people decide with full information. The group that knows it is $600 in March is still your friend group in May. The one that finds out in April is not.
