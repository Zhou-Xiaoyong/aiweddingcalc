Five days before a November 28 wedding, $20,451 of a $34,200 budget has not left your account. That is 60% of the wedding, or $232 for every one of the 88 guests who sits down on Saturday. Your wedding final payment deadline is Wednesday, and none of it is a surprise. All of it is due inside the next 96 hours.

So the question stops being how much you owe. It becomes which day the money has to clear. Wednesday, November 25, before 2 p.m. Not Thursday, because the Federal Reserve is closed. Not Friday, because the caterer's office runs on two people and so does the bank's wire desk.

Here is what is still owed, four ways to pay it priced against each other, and the order to pay if the account will not cover all of it.

Deposits are the easy part. They are 25% to 50%, they went out months ago, and they make a wedding feel mostly paid for in October. The balances cluster into the last ten days, when vendors finally know their costs.

Why Your Wedding Final Payment Deadline Is Wednesday, Not Friday

Count the business days backwards from Saturday, November 28. Friday the 27th is a business day on paper and a ghost town in practice. Thursday the 26th is Thanksgiving, a Federal Reserve holiday, so no wire moves and no branch opens. That leaves Wednesday the 25th, Tuesday the 24th, Monday the 23rd, and Friday the 20th.

A contract that says "five business days prior" set your deadline at Friday, November 20. It has already gone by. One that says "two business days" means Wednesday. One that says "72 hours" means Wednesday noon. Every phrasing converges on the same morning, which is why the guarantee conversation and the payment conversation are really one call, as the 72-hour guest count guide explains.

What happens after Wednesday is not a penalty. It is a delay, and the delay is the cost. A wire instructed after 2 p.m. misses the last Fed window and posts the next business day, which on the Friday after Thanksgiving can mean Monday, November 30, two days after you are married. A check deposited Friday morning clears Monday at the earliest. Either way the vendor's system shows an unpaid balance on the day of the event.

Cashier's checks have a smaller window than people expect. Most branches stop issuing them at 3 p.m., and the check still has to physically arrive. An overnight letter sent Wednesday before the carrier's 5 p.m. cutoff is scheduled for Thursday, which is Thanksgiving, which means no delivery. It lands Friday if the vendor's office is open at all. Confirm that before you pay $28 for the envelope.

Four Ways to Move $6,930, Priced

The caterer's balance is the biggest transfer of the week, and the method changes its price by $208 and its arrival date by four days.

Method Cost on $6,930 Daily cap Clears
Credit card $208 at 3% Your limit Instantly, less the fee
Zelle from your bank $0 $2,000 to $3,500 Two to four days, in pieces
Cashier's check, overnighted $36 to $43 None Friday, if they are open
Wire before 2 p.m. $25 to $35 None Same day, Wednesday

Zelle looks free and is not, in the way that matters. Daily send limits at the four largest banks run $2,000 to $3,500 to a new recipient, and several hold a first transfer for review. Moving $6,930 takes two to four days you do not have. ACH bill-pay is cheaper and slower, typically three business days.

The wire is the only method that lands the same day, and it costs $25 to $35 outgoing unless a premium account waives it. Get the account name, ABA number, and account number in writing, then read the digits back over the phone. A misdirected wire is recoverable only with the receiving bank's cooperation, and that takes weeks.

One warning specific to this week. If an email arrives Wednesday saying the caterer changed banks, treat it as fraud until a human voice on the number printed on your contract says otherwise. Payment-diversion scams target exactly this window, when the balance is large and the person paying it is out of time to verify anything.

The 3% Card Fee Costs More Than the Tip You Are Debating

Put the whole remaining $20,451 on a rewards card and the surcharges come to about $613. The maitre d' tip you have debated for a week is $150 to $200. The fee is three times the decision you already spent seven days on.

The surcharge is legal in most states, has to be disclosed before you pay, and under card network rules cannot exceed what the merchant is charged, with 3% as the working ceiling. Massachusetts requires the same difference to appear as a cash discount instead, which is why 3% shows up on invoices under two names. Ask which one you are being charged.

Where the fee hurts is the float. A card that holds the balance until the statement closes in 25 days is 3% for three and a half weeks of borrowed time, which annualizes near 44%. A personal loan is cheaper. So is a written five-day extension, and that ask costs one phone call.

There is one reason to accept the fee. A card payment carries a dispute right under federal rules, 60 days from the statement showing the charge. That is worth 3% on a deposit you might need back, and not worth it on a caterer who already fed your guests.

Cash You Need in Hand by Tuesday

Three of Saturday's payments cannot be swiped. The officiant is handed an envelope before the ceremony, the hair and makeup team is paid at the chair, and the DJ gets the balance before the first dance. On this wedding that is $3,912: $650, $2,462, and $800, plus $240 for two shuttle drivers at $120 each.

Getting $3,912 out of a bank this week takes more planning than it sounds. ATM limits run $300 to $1,000 a day depending on the bank and the account, so the machine is not the answer. A teller can hand over that amount from the drawer, but past $2,000 in specific denominations it is a one to two business day request. Thursday the branch is closed. Friday it closes early and the line is long. Tuesday, November 24, is the day to walk in.

Ask for the denominations you will actually hand over: hundreds and twenties for the makeup balance, thirties and hundreds for the officiant, small bills for the DJ and the drivers. Then write the name and amount on each envelope and split them into a morning stack and a reception stack. Labeled envelopes are the difference between a calm morning and a conversation in a parking lot about who has been paid.

Who Gets Paid First If the Money Is Short

Pay in reverse order of who can halt the day, not in order of invoice date. Vendors holding equipment or buying perishables this week can. The ones who show up and perform cannot un-perform.

First, rentals at $1,590. The truck loads Friday morning. Unpaid, it turns around, and you are missing 88 chairs and a tent. Nothing else on this list is that binary.

Second, the caterer at $6,930. Food is ordered Tuesday and cooked Wednesday. A caterer who has not been paid by Wednesday night has a real reason to trim the order, and they will describe it as a portioning adjustment rather than a cut.

Third, the florist at $1,450. Same logic, smaller number. Wholesale flowers are bought Monday and Tuesday, and a market that runs out of a variety does not restock it by Friday. Our Thanksgiving flower cost breakdown covers how fast that market closes.

Fourth, the venue at $4,100. You are already inside the building for the rehearsal, so their only move is a fee, not a cancellation. Expect 1.5% a month and ask for the waiver anyway.

Last, everyone paid in cash on the day. Hair and makeup, the DJ, the officiant, the drivers. They work first and invoice after, and a promise of Monday is normally accepted. Put it in a text so nobody is guessing at four in the afternoon.

The photographer's balance is not on this list because it cleared on the 14th, which was the right order. Photographers keep their files until the invoice is paid, and an edit does not speed up because you are owed a favor. If a vendor walks this week, the replacement cost breakdown covers it, and the emergency fund calculator says whether the fund absorbs it.

Run all of it and the week costs $817, or $9.28 a guest, on a wedding that averages $389 a head. None of it appears in a budget template. All of it comes out of the same account paying for the flowers.

The cheaper route is unglamorous. Wire the venue and the caterer on Wednesday at $30 each, pay everything else by check or cash, and skip the card entirely. That is $60 against $613, and it costs one hour at a bank. Against the $34,200 total the difference is 1.6%, which is $7 a guest, which is a signature cocktail per person at the prices in our cocktail cost guide.
