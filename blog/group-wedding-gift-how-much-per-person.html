Twelve people, one $400 stand mixer, and a group chat that goes silent the second someone asks what everyone is putting in. That is the group wedding gift problem in miniature: the gift is easy, the split is not.

Here is the number most groups are looking for: $20 to $75 per person in 2026, with $15 as the practical floor and the individual gift norm as the ceiling. Which end of that range you land on depends almost entirely on two things, headcount and how well the group knows the couple, and barely at all on how expensive the wedding is. For context, the average US wedding runs about $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study). Nobody getting married at that number is counting on your group chat to cover the catering.

Group gifts split along the same relationship lines as individual gifts, only discounted. Each person gives less than they would alone because the pooled total is doing the work:

The bridal party line carries an asterisk. Bridesmaids and groomsmen have already paid for attire, travel, showers, and a rehearsal dinner contribution, which on the low end is several hundred dollars each. A wedding party that pools $75 a head on top of that is being generous, not stingy. If you are the one organizing it, ask the party before you set the number.

For a baseline on solo giving, our coworker gift amounts guide runs $50–$75 for a friendly colleague and $100–$150 if you attend. A group should never ask more per person than that.

Run the Split Backwards

Most groups get this wrong in the same direction. They start by asking "what should I put in," collect a fuzzy pile of $20s and $50s, and then discover the total buys a mid-range blender. Start at the other end.

  • Pick the gift or the cash target first. A $500 figure, or a specific registry item that costs $480.
  • Divide by confirmed contributors, not by people who were invited, not by the group chat's member count, and definitely not by people who said "maybe."
  • Round up to a clean number. Nine people splitting $500 pay $55.50 each. Round to $60 and the pool is $540, which covers the item, the card, and $15 of shipping.

Two rules keep the result defensible. The ceiling rule: never contribute more in a group than you would have given alone. A twelve-person office pool that works out to $85 a head is not a group gift anymore, it is a collection plate with extra steps, and the right move is to buy a cheaper item. The floor rule: below about $15 a head, stop pooling. Sign a card, put in $10 if you want, and skip the Venmo thread. The admin cost exceeds the gift.

If you want a starting point rather than a formula, the wedding gift calculator returns the solo range for your relationship; divide that by three and you have a comfortable group number.

What Group Size Does to the Check

Headcount is the lever most groups never pull. The same $500 gift costs $100 a head at five people and $17 a head at thirty:

30$7$12$17

Read the bottom row carefully. At thirty contributors, a $500 gift asks $17 from each person, which is under the floor and awkward to collect from thirty individual people. Groups that size work better with a fixed ask ($20 each, whatever it totals) than with a target they reverse-engineer. Fixed asks also survive the inevitable dropouts: if six of thirty never pay, a $20 ask still delivers $480.

What the Group Should Actually Buy

Registry items are the natural target because the couple already said they want them. Realistic price points from a typical registry: a stand mixer around $400, a three-piece luggage set $450–$650, a knife block $200–$300, a single place setting of good dinnerware $60–$150 that eight people can buy together, a vacuum $300–$500.

Cash pooling is simpler and lands better with most couples. Our gift data shows about 80% of couples prefer cash, and a group contribution of $25–$50 each stacks into something real: eight people at $40 is $320, enough to fund a honeymoon excursion or cover a chunk of a rental car.

What not to do: buy the $1,200 espresso machine as a surprise because it looks impressive in a group chat. If it was not on the registry, the couple now owns a $1,200 appliance, a counter they have to clear, and a thank-you note obligation toward twenty people. Registry presence is the signal that the object is wanted.

Collecting Without Becoming the Villain

The person who starts the pool inherits the least pleasant job at the wedding that isn't theirs. Five habits make it survivable.

  • Put the amount and the deadline in the same message. "$40 each, in by Friday the 9th, Venmo to @name" gets a yes or a no. "Let me know what you'd like to contribute" gets silence.
  • Use one platform. Venmo personal transfers and Zelle are free. Venmo's instant transfer to a bank costs 1.75% with a 25-cent minimum and $25 cap, so let the money land over the standard one to three business days instead of paying to move it faster.
  • Avoid splitting PayPal across twelve people. Goods and Services runs 2.99% plus $0.49 per payment, so twelve separate $25 payments cost about $14.85 in fees against $9.46 for one $300 payment. Collect into one pot, or use a free transfer app.
  • Do not front the whole gift on your card and hope. If the item has to be ordered before the money arrives, say exactly that: "I'm ordering Friday, so please send by Thursday."
  • Keep a list. Someone will ask in March whether they paid, and the answer should not be a scroll through Venmo history.

One more: never post a running tally in the group thread. The moment people can see who has and hasn't paid, the gift stops being a gift and becomes a scoreboard.

The Five Awkward Cases

Someone cannot afford their share. Lower the target for everyone and buy something smaller. Do not organize a quiet subsidy and do not say "we'll cover you, pay us back later," which converts a gift into a debt. If the collector absorbs a share without telling anyone, the problem disappears, which is usually the best outcome available.

Someone wants to give much more. Take it privately and do not announce it. The gift is from the group. A conspicuous big giver makes everyone else's $30 feel like a failure, and the couple ends up thanking one person for a present twelve people paid for.

You only know one half of the couple. Contribute at the level of the person you actually know, and put both names on the card. Your relationship is with one of them; the etiquette runs through that person.

Someone never responds. Send one reminder, then let it go. A third message costs more socially than the $40 you were chasing, and the group will remember which of the two you chose.

A plus-one was added to the thread. A date who has never met the couple is not a contributor. If they want to chip in, fine, but nobody should be asked on their behalf.

Group Wedding Gift: Registry Item vs. Cash Pool

Registry group-gift tools on the major platforms work by partial contribution: each person puts in what they want, and the item ships only once it is fully funded. That solves the split math and creates two new problems. A $400 item stuck at 70% funded does not ship, and it can sit there for months while the couple assumes it is coming. And a pledge is not a payment, so the group can believe the gift is handled while the balance quietly never closes.

Cash has neither failure mode. The money arrives, the couple spends it on whatever the wedding actually needed, and there is nothing to track. The tradeoff is that cash does not photograph well in a group chat, and some groups genuinely want to hand over an object. If that is your group, pick a registry item with a price the pool can clear in one pass rather than one that needs a second round of asking.

Who Gets the Thank-You Card

The couple owes a thank-you to every person who paid, not to the group as an entity. One card addressed to "the team" is acceptable only if every contributor is named inside it, spelled correctly. With twelve people in the pool, that is twelve names, and getting them wrong is the one mistake guests actually notice.

So the collector's last job is the useful one: after the wedding, send the couple a plain list of who contributed and how their name is spelled. It costs one message and saves an hour of guessing. Couples planning the backlog can size it with the thank-you card calculator, which budgets cards at guest count plus 10–15% for gifts from people who did not attend. A group gift adds names without adding guests, which is exactly the gap that buffer covers.

If you are on the giving side and doing this four or five times in one season, the per-person number is your real budget line. Five groups at $40 is $200, and that is money sitting next to your own savings goal, not inside someone else's wedding. Track it the way you would any other recurring expense, using the wedding budget calculator if you are planning your own wedding in the same year and the savings calculator if you are not.
