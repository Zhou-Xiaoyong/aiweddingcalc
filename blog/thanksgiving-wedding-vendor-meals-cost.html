Eight. On an 88-guest wedding, eight vendor meals go out to people who are not on your guest list: two photographers, a videographer, a DJ, three planners, and the photo booth attendant. Your 88-guest wedding feeds 96 people.

The bill for those eight plates runs $562 to $840 depending on whether your contract charges a boxed rate or a full guest entrée with service charge and tax on top. Skip that question and a Thanksgiving date makes it $881.

Your count locked Wednesday at noon. Four of the eight are already paid for inside the 5% overage the caterer builds into every quote, which is the only piece of good news on this page.

The wedding below is 88 guests, a $34,200 budget, $105 a head for catering all in, and a hotel ballroom on Saturday, November 28.

Who Needs a Vendor Meal at Your Wedding

One test decides it: is this person on site across a meal service? Not how long they work, and not how much they cost. A string trio that plays the ceremony and the cocktail hour and loads out at 6:30 does not eat dinner. A photo booth attendant who clocks in at 7 does.

Dinner here is 7:00 to 8:15. Anyone whose call time lands after the entrée is cleared, or whose wrap comes before it is served, drops off the list.

The officiant is the one most couples double-count. On this wedding he is seated at table four and already inside the 88. If you hired him through an agency, the meal is usually built into the fee, which means a ninth plate pays for dinner twice. Ask the agency, not the caterer.

The catering crew of thirteen is the other trap. Crew meals come out of the labor rate, and no caterer should sell you thirteen vendor meals at the guest rate. On Thanksgiving week some houses add a "holiday crew plate" line to cover staff who would normally eat the family meal after service. That line is legitimate. It is also negotiable, and at $85 a plate for thirteen people it is $1,105 of new money, which is more than the eight vendor meals on this page.

The shuttle drivers are simpler: coach companies bill driver meals at $30 to $40 a head and it is already in the quote. Do not add them. If a driver has been looping for four hours he will take a box, and he will eat it in the coach between runs.

What a Vendor Meal Costs, Three Ways

Caterers bill vendor meals three different ways, and the difference on eight plates is $278. That is 0.8% of a $34,200 budget, small enough to ignore and large enough that nobody wants to find it on a Monday invoice.

Hosts usually picture the middle row and get the third. A hotel that already has 88 covers plated does not want to invent a sandwich service, so the default line on the addendum reads "vendor meals at the per-person rate." You are agreeing to $840 when $562 was available, and the only question that separates them is the one nobody asks: does the service charge apply to a meal served at a folding table in a hallway?

There is a fourth arrangement worth knowing, because it changes the count rather than the price. Some venues plated vendor meals at a guest table, which turns a vendor into a seat. On this wedding that is a half table, and the seating chart math for a twelfth table puts a table at $242 before food. Feed them in the green room. They will not mind, and the photographer will be back on the floor before the toasts end.

The one plate to never cut is the photographer's. Coverage on this wedding runs to 10 p.m., and the last ninety minutes happen after eight hours of standing. A photographer who eats at 7:20 while your guests eat is working the sparkler exit at 9:40. One who eats at 9:45 is not.

Why a Thanksgiving Vendor Meal Costs More

A boxed vendor meal is $65 on a June Saturday and $75 to $95 on Thanksgiving week, because the kitchen is running a holiday menu with holiday pay behind it. On this wedding it lands at $85. Four things drive that, and only one of them is the food.

  • Nothing else is open. On a normal Saturday a planner can send a PA to a deli at 4 p.m. On Thanksgiving the deli is closed, the grocery counter shut at noon, and the only kitchen that can feed eight people is the one you already hired. That is a monopoly, and it is priced like one.
  • No family meal. Catering crews normally eat at 10:45 p.m. from the kitchen after service, and that meal is the caterer's cost. Thanksgiving week the hotel releases the crew at 9, so the holiday plate gets built before service instead. Ask whether it is in the labor rate. It should be. A quote that adds it as a line item is a quote you can push back on.
  • The hot line closes at 9. Vendor meals have to fire with the guest entrées at 7:15, not be ordered at 8:45. An order that late comes out as whatever survived the banquet, which is how a photographer ends up eating dinner rolls at 10 p.m.
  • The staff is on a holiday schedule. Venue and catering offices run skeleton crews on Thursday. A count added after the deadline may be refused rather than upcharged, and a refusal is much harder to solve at 5 p.m. than a $207 premium.

Read the third row from the bottom and the number looks like nothing. Two dollars and thirty-five cents a guest. A passed champagne at the cocktail hour costs more than that per person, and nobody argues about the champagne.

Read the second row from the bottom instead. You are feeding 96 people for $9,914, which is $103.27 a head, less than the $105 guest average because a box is cheaper than a plate. The cheapest food at the wedding is the food nobody planned for.

If your date is the Thursday rather than the Saturday, the premium is different and the deadline is worse. A Thursday wedding runs into the day itself: the venue kitchen is on a holiday menu, the crew is on holiday pay, and several states require 1.5x for the hours worked. Our cost breakdown for the four Thanksgiving dates bills a 120-guest version of one room at $37,054 on Thursday against $30,471 on Sunday, and the vendor meal line sits inside that gap.

The Count Was Due Yesterday. What Still Moves.

The guest count locked Wednesday at noon, which is the standard 72-hour rule for a Saturday wedding. Vendor meals are not guest meals, and caterers treat them differently, so there is still room. Six moves, cheapest first.

  • The 5% overage. A caterer holding 92 plates for 88 guests has four spares. Ask for four of the eight vendor meals to come out of that buffer and most captains say yes, because handing over four boxes is easier than explaining a $674 addition on Monday. That is $337 of the bill gone without a negotiation.
  • Boxed instead of plated. $674 against $840. The greens come in a container instead of on china, and the eight people eating them will not notice the difference, because they are eating standing up in a service corridor.
  • Waive the service charge. Worth $112 on this wedding. No service is performed at a folding table, and the argument is easy to make in one sentence before the count locks. After it locks, you are asking for a credit.
  • Check the officiant's contract. If the agency fee includes a meal, you are ordering a ninth plate for a plate that already exists.
  • Check the planner's scope. A third assistant the planner brought without telling you is the planner's invoice, not yours. Read the scope of work before approving the plate.
  • The grocery deli, only if you are not at a hotel. Eight boxes at $14 to $18 is $144, and it has to be picked up the day before, which for a Saturday wedding means Friday. At a hotel this does not work: most contracts forbid outside food and enforce it with a $250 to $500 fee, which is more than the $530 you saved. At a barn or a county park with an approved caterer and no exclusivity clause, it works fine.

Put the ask in one message, with a number and a deadline: "Eight vendor meals, boxed, service charge waived, four pulled from the overage. Can you confirm by 4 p.m. today?" That sentence costs $280 instead of $674, and it takes ninety seconds to send.

If your count already cleared and nothing will move, order the eight boxes anyway and pay the $881. A hungry DJ plays a short set, a hungry photographer leaves at 9:30, and a hungry planner stops solving problems. The food is the cheapest insurance in the building. The emergency fund calculator sizes the cash you should be holding for exactly this kind of Thursday addition, and the Wednesday closing-time guide covers the errands that cannot be fixed with money.

For an 88-guest holiday wedding, put $674 in the catering line the day you sign, specify boxed, and write "service charge does not apply to vendor meals" into the addendum. If the count already closed, the ask above brings the day-of addition down to $280. Either number is smaller than the cost of a vendor who spends your reception looking for a sandwich.
