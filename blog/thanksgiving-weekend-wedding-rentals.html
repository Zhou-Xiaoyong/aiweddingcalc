A ballroom two miles from the church wants $4,800 and hands over tables, chairs, china, heat, and restrooms. The barn your cousin found wants $6,200 and hands over a gravel floor and two 20-amp outlets. Which one is cheaper is the question Thanksgiving weekend wedding rentals exist to answer.

For 88 guests, the barn loses by $5,805 the moment a truck arrives with tables, chairs, china, linens, a dance floor, and bistro lighting. Put a heated tent on the family field beside it and the gap becomes $10,839. Nothing about the room changed except who owns the furniture.

Three rooms. Same 88 people. One of them costs $54.55 a head before food and one costs $193.91.

The ballroom number is real, with one string attached. A $4,800 room fee only exists because you are booking their kitchen, and most of these contracts carry a food and beverage minimum around $12,500. Thanksgiving Weekend Wedding Rentals: The Cheap Part Is the Tent

Of the $15,239, the tent itself is $5,640. Sidewalls add $504 and engineered staking for a November wind adds $425. That is $6,569 for a structure, or 43% of the invoice. The other $8,670 is everything you put inside it.

Couples planning a field wedding almost always price the tent first, because it is the visible thing. Then the heater quote shocks them, and the restroom trailer shocks them again, and by the third shock they start cutting furniture. Cutting chairs on a 40x60 is how you end up with 88 people standing.

Here is the part a rental company will not volunteer. A 40x60 frame tent at $2.35 a square foot is a fair price in most markets. A 40x60 clear-span, which removes the center poles and gives you a clean sightline, runs $4.50 to $7.00 a square foot, or $10,800 to $16,800 for the same footprint. If your aisle and your first dance both need a clear center line, that is the line that breaks the budget, not the heaters.

Sizing the Heat for a 30-Degree Night

The sizing rule is 35 to 40 BTU per square foot. A 2,400 sq ft tent needs 84,000 to 96,000 BTU, and one 170,000 BTU indirect-fired heater appears to cover it with room to spare. It does not, and the reason is distribution rather than capacity.

A single heater builds a warm zone about 20 feet across and leaves the far end of the tent at whatever the outside air is. You need two units on a 2,400 sq ft tent and three when the forecast drops below 30 degrees, which is what a late-November Saturday in the northern half of the country usually delivers after dark. Three heaters rent for about $215 each.

Propane is the line nobody quotes. A 170,000 BTU heater burns roughly 1.86 gallons an hour, and a 100-lb cylinder holds 23.6 gallons, so one tank runs about 12 hours. At $105 a refill, three heaters is $315 for the night, and you should plan a second set if the tent goes up Wednesday and stands through Thanksgiving. Below 20 degrees a 100-lb cylinder struggles to vaporize fast enough to feed a burner that size, which is why rental companies bring a 250-gallon tank instead of a rack of cylinders.

Two habits make the difference between a warm tent and a cold one. Start the heaters at 3 p.m., not at 5, because you are pushing 40-degree air out of a fabric box and that takes hours. And put the sidewall door on the downwind side, since every opening dumps the room.

What Thanksgiving Weekend Wedding Rentals Bill in a Four-Day Week

Thanksgiving weekend wedding rentals do not bill like a normal Saturday, because the calendar around them does not work like a normal week. Thanksgiving Day falls on Thursday, November 26, which means the tent crew wants Wednesday the 25th, the crew is off Thursday, and pickup lands Monday the 30th. Five days on site for a four-hour event.

Most companies bill a Friday-to-Monday weekend as a single rental period. A Wednesday install with a Monday pickup usually bills as two, and the holiday handling fee sits on top of that.

One more calendar trap. Friday, November 27 is the busiest delivery day of the year for rental companies, because every holiday party and storefront install wants that day. If your vendor suggests pushing the install from Wednesday to Friday to save the extra days, understand that yours is the delivery most likely to be bumped to Saturday morning. A tent that goes up four hours before the ceremony is a tent with no heat in it.

Restrooms, Power, and the Fork Count

Two stations for 88 guests is the number on every quote, and it is wrong. The working rule is one station per 50 guests for a four-hour event, tightened to one per 40 when there is a bar. Eighty-eight guests with an open bar needs three, and a two-station trailer will have a line from 8 p.m. until the band stops. The third station adds about $400.

Power is usually oversold in the other direction. Add up what actually draws current: three heater blowers at 5 amps each, 320 feet of LED bistro at 4, a DJ at 15, and a caterer's holding cabinets at 30. That is under 70 amps, and a 12 kW towable generator with fuel covers it for about $575. A 25 kW quote is sized for a mobile kitchen you are not renting.

Then the fork count. Rental agreements bill lost and broken china at roughly three times replacement cost, not at the rental rate. Six missing forks is not $4.50, it is $22.50, and it appears on a card you left on file two months after the wedding. Count the crates in with the crew and count them out again, on paper, both times. Caterers break glass; that is what they do.

Sunset at 4:40 and the Lighting Bill

On November 28 the sun is down before 5 p.m. almost everywhere in the lower 48. A 4 p.m. ceremony means you are lighting the room for its last hour of daylight and then for another six hours of dark, which is five hours more lighting than a June wedding needs from the same tent.

A 40x60 has a 200-foot perimeter, so 320 linear feet of bistro is about one and a half passes at $2.10 a foot, plus a dimmer and pole kit. That is the $740 line in the table. The $920 figure is what you actually spend once you add two tower uplights and path lighting to the parking area, because 88 people crossing a hay field at 10:30 in the dark is a liability claim rather than a design decision.

Test the light before the wedding, not during it. String a run at 5 p.m. on a weekday in the same month and look at it from the far corner of the tent. LED bistro at 2,700K reads romantic in a photo and dim on a face, and one uplight aimed wrong turns the whole ceiling the color of a hallway.

Best Picks for a November 28 Wedding

Three honest recommendations, priced per guest on the venue side, with the trade each one makes.

  • Under 100 guests, one contract, zero logistics: the ballroom. $54.55 a head, and one phone number to call when the heat fails. You give up control of the menu, the room's look, and the end time. Worth it if your list is small and your planning time is not.
  • A building with a roof, heat, and plumbing already in it: the barn or grange hall. $120.51 a head, $4,405 of rentals, and you need a Tuesday and a Wednesday free to receive them. This is the best value on the list, and the only one where a rental company mistake is survivable.
  • The location is the point, and nothing else will do: the tent. $177.72 a head as built, $193.91 in Thanksgiving week. Add a rain plan, a generator, a 3 p.m. heat start, and a person whose only job on Saturday is the sidewall door.

If the tent is not the point, do not build one in November. The gap between $120.51 and $177.72 is $5,034 for 88 guests, and that money buys a shuttle, a welcome dinner, and a hotel block that people actually use. All three of those show up in a guest's memory of the weekend. A rented dance floor does not.

Before you sign anything, get three numbers in writing: the exact rental period dates, the holiday handling fee as a dollar figure, and the delivery window. A quote missing those three is a brochure. Compare it against the cost of moving the wedding to Friday or Sunday, since a Friday date can pull the whole build inside a single rental period and erase most of that $1,425, then check the attendance math for this weekend before you commit to 88 seats on a rented floor.
