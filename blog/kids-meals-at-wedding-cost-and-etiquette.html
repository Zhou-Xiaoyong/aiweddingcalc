Twelve children under ten on a 120-guest list is ordinary. The invoice line they generate is not. At $95 a head for a plated dinner, those twelve seats cost $1,509 once the 22% service charge and 8.5% sales tax are applied. Kids meals at a wedding typically run $15 to $35, which puts the same twelve seats at $445 and leaves $1,064 in your account.

That is the entire financial argument, and it fits in one sentence. The complications sit around it: which children qualify, what your venue can charge, and how to print a child's portion on an RSVP card without telling a guest their kid is a line item.

What Kids Meals at a Wedding Actually Cost

Most caterers price a child plate at 25% to 35% of the adult dinner, and the food itself is not why. Chicken tenders, mac and cheese, and a fruit cup cost a kitchen $4 to $6 in ingredients. The rest of the $28 covers the server, the dishwasher, and the place setting.

All-in figures assume a 22% service charge and 8.5% sales tax on both plates, which is how most contracts read. The tax and tip calculator will model your state's actual rate.

The buffet row deserves a warning. A seven-year-old at a buffet eats roughly 40% of what an adult eats, so a $65 per-person buffet puts about $26 of food on that child's plate. Paying a $18 child rate is a fair trade. Paying the full $86 all-in for a child who eats three bites of pasta is not, and that happens any time a venue has no child rate on the menu.

The Age Cutoffs Behind the Price

Every catering contract defines a child in writing, and the definition is worth more than the price. Two bands cover almost every venue in the country.

Age What You Pay What to Confirm
Under 2No charge, usually no seatHigh chair availability, and the $8 to $12 some kitchens charge for a baby plate
2 to 10Child rate, $15 to $35Whether the rate is a menu line or a discount applied at invoice
11 and 12Adult plate at roughly half of venuesAsk for 12 and under in writing; this band is negotiated, not fixed
13 and upAdult plate, no exceptionsNothing. Order them the salmon.

The 11 and 12 band is where the money hides. On a list with three eleven-year-olds, moving the definition from 10-and-under to 12-and-under saves $201 all-in. It also produces a better dinner, because a twelve-year-old who has been served chicken tenders at a formal wedding will remember it longer than you will.

One more detail that surprises parents: a child under two eats free because there is no plate, not because there is no person. The room's posted occupancy count includes them, and if you want a chair rather than a lap, some venues will count the seat at the adult rate.

Our catering calculator prices every seat at the adult rate, because it takes a guest count and not a birth date. A plated dinner at the standard tier comes back at $95 a head whether that head is 42 or 6.

That is the right default for a first estimate, since most couples run the number before the RSVPs are in. It is the wrong number for your final comparison. If 12 of your 120 guests are under ten, take 12 × $67 off the food line before you put two quotes side by side. On a 120-guest plated dinner that is $804, and it is the difference between two caterers who looked $800 apart.

The clean way to build it: run the guest list calculator twice, once with all 120 and once with 108, then price the 12 children separately. Keep the two counts visible in your spreadsheet, because the caterer will ask for a single number at the 72-hour guarantee and you want to know which 12 names are in it.

The same arithmetic separates a plus-one from a child on the guest list. One costs $126 all-in, the other $37. Treating them as the same line item is how guest lists get trimmed in the wrong places.

The Minimum That Eats the Saving

Before you spend an afternoon negotiating a child rate, find out whether your venue has a food and beverage minimum. Many do, and the minimum is calculated on the food and beverage subtotal, before tax and service charge.

Run the trap with real numbers. Your 120 guests split into 108 adults at $95 and 12 children at $28. Food total: $10,260 plus $336, or $10,596. The venue's minimum is $12,000, so the invoice shows $12,000 and a $1,404 shortfall line.

Now price the same wedding with no child rate at all. 120 × $95 is $11,400, the shortfall is $600, and the invoice still shows $12,000. The child plates changed nothing. The $804 stayed with the venue.

So the order of operations matters. Get the minimum in writing first, then look at your total. If you clear it comfortably, the child rate is money in your pocket. If you do not, spend the afternoon on the guest count instead, because the count is what moves you across the line. Kids Meal Etiquette on the Invitation and RSVP

Almost every awkward moment in this category comes from ambiguity rather than from children. Four rules cover it.

  • Name the people you are inviting. "The Smith Family" invites everyone in the house. Naming the two adults on the inner envelope excludes children without a sentence anyone has to read twice.
  • Put the child plate on the RSVP card, not in a cover note. A meal line that reads "Chicken, Salmon, Vegetarian, Child's Plate" does the work quietly, and add a blank for "children attending, ages" so your caterer gets ages rather than a guess.
  • Offer the adult plate to any child eight or older. A child plate is a portion size, not a demotion. Parents of a nine-year-old will usually take the salmon and split it.
  • Never mention the price to a guest. The moment a parent hears "the kids meals are $28 each," the invitation becomes a bill. Keep the number in your spreadsheet.

The Kids Table That Works

A kids table works when the ages are close and the count is small. It fails when a four-year-old is seated with a twelve-year-old and an eight-year-old is asked to behave like a ten-year-old.

  • Five to ten, six to eight seats. That band shares a bedtime and an attention span. Above eight children, run two tables or hire a sitter.
  • Place it near a parent, not near the speakers. Ten feet from an aunt is supervision. Ten feet from the DJ is a light show with a floor full of children.
  • Keep it away from the bar and off the path to the exit. You want adults walking past the table, not children walking behind the bar.
  • Give one older child a job. An eleven-year-old as table captain with a disposable camera and a plate of crayons buys you 40 minutes of quiet for about $15.
  • Fire the kids plates first. Ask the caterer to send them ten minutes before the adults. This is nearly always free and it prevents the 6:45 p.m. collapse.

Activity kits run $8 to $15 each, so $96 to $180 for a table of twelve. Skip the candy and anything with small parts. Stickers and a coloring sheet hold attention longer than sugar, and sugar ends at 8 p.m. with everyone paying for it.

Plan the exit too. Most families with young children leave after the cake, so if your minute-by-minute timeline cuts the cake before the dance floor opens, the departure wave handles itself.

When a Sitter Costs Less Than the Plates

Sitters in most US metros charge $25 to $40 an hour with a four-hour minimum. Two sitters for 12 children runs $200 to $320 for the evening. Add $144 in activity kits and you have spent $344 to $464.

The child plates saved you $804. The sitter is covered twice over, and what you bought is a reception where the parents get to eat.

Ask the venue first. A hotel with a suite can host a movie and a pizza delivery for $120, and some venue insurance policies require a background-checked sitter once a group passes six children. That is a venue requirement, not a law, and it varies enough that a phone call is faster than a search.

A sitter does not reduce the plate count. The twelve meals still get ordered and billed. You are buying calm, not savings, and if the budget is tight, the sitter is the line to cut.

Five Questions for Your Caterer

Send these in one email before you sign. Every answer changes a number on the invoice.

  1. How does the contract define a child? Get the age in writing, and push for 12 and under.
  2. Is the child rate a menu line or a discount? A menu line survives a change of coordinator. A discount gets forgotten at invoicing.
  3. Does the service charge apply to child plates? It usually does, which is why the $28 plate lands at $37. Some caterers waive it on children under six.
  4. Do children count toward the food and beverage minimum? If yes, price the minimum before you celebrate the saving.
  5. Are children included in the guaranteed count? They should be, and the number is due 72 hours out along with the adults.

One more, if you are serving a buffet: ask whether the kitchen will plate children's portions back in the kitchen. Some will, at no charge, and it prevents a seven-year-old from returning to the line four times.

If You Are Not Inviting Children

An adults-only wedding is a normal choice in 2026, and the couples who regret it usually regret the delivery rather than the decision. Three things make it land.

  • Say it once, on the website. A single line in the FAQ, phrased as a fact: "Our celebration is adults-only. We can't wait to celebrate with you."
  • Call the people it affects most. Siblings, the wedding party, and anyone traveling more than two hours with a toddler.
  • Help with childcare if you can. A sitter at the hotel for $150 to $300 turns a declined RSVP into an accepted one, and it is cheaper than the two plates that family would have used.

Expect 10% to 15% of out-of-town parents to decline. That is not a snub, it is a Thursday-night babysitting problem. Budget the decline into your count rather than arguing with it.

If you are somewhere in between, the middle path beats both extremes: invite the children, put them at one table with a sitter, and feed them at 6 p.m. before the toasts. You get the family photos, the parents get a dance floor, and the cost is about $37 a child.
