A full-service planner quotes 12% of a $34,200 wedding, which comes to $4,104. A day-of coordinator eight miles away quotes $1,800 flat, and both emails promise to handle the day so you can enjoy it. The wedding planner vs day of coordinator gap between those two numbers is roughly $2,300, and what it buys is eleven months of work that appears on neither contract.

This decision is not really about the wedding day. It is about how many contracts you will sign with nobody reading the fine print beside you. A day-of coordinator's first working hour happens after the last signature.

Whether that difference is worth paying comes down to four numbers: your guest count, your vendor count, whether the venue is a raw space, and how many of your own evenings you can afford to lose to email.

What Each Service Level Costs in 2026

Four job titles get sold as if they were interchangeable, and the pricing model changes at each one. The national average wedding sits at $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study), so the percentage column is easy to check against your own number.

Our planner cost calculator uses 12% for full service, 6% for partial, a flat $2,500 for month-of and $1,500 for day-of. All four land inside what planners actually quote in 2026, with one arithmetic wrinkle covered further down: the percentage gets applied to whatever budget you type in, and that budget normally includes the fee.

Watch the word "day-of." Almost nobody sells a true day-of anymore, because a coordinator who meets your florist for the first time at 8 am cannot run a load-in schedule. What the market calls day-of starts four to six weeks out, when you hand over the vendor list, the contracts and the half-finished timeline. Ask the question in plain terms: how many weeks before the wedding do you take over?

Put the price next to the hours and the ranking inverts.

The day-of coordinator is the cheapest line on the contract and the most expensive hour you will buy. Those 22 hours break down roughly like this: two venue walkthroughs at three hours each, six to eight hours building the run-of-show and confirming vendors, six to eight hours of email in the final fortnight, and ten to twelve hours on the day itself, from the 8 am load-in to the last rental counted at midnight.

The planner's 200 hours sit in months you have not started thinking about. Twelve venue tours, thirty vendor conversations, four contract reviews, a design deck, a floor plan, forty hours of email, and the same wedding-day shift the coordinator works. Split the fee by the hours and you are paying $20.50 an hour for the person who shapes the wedding or $81.80 for the person who executes it. Neither is a bad buy. They are different products that share a job title.

Read the third row twice, because contract review is the line that pays for a planner and the one thing a coordinator cannot sell you. By the time they arrive, every signature is dry. A 20% service charge that never reaches the staff, a five-hour reception block that bills $200 an hour after 10 pm, an F&B minimum that does not credit your site fee: all findable before signing, all permanent after.

The 12% Fee Is Charged on a Number You Control

Percentage pricing hides an arithmetic problem. If the total wedding budget is $34,200 and the planner charges 12%, the fee is not $4,104, because $4,104 of that budget has to pay the planner first. Solve it correctly and the fee is 12% of ($34,200 minus the fee), which lands at $3,664. Most quotes will not correct this for you, and most couples never check.

The model also breaks at both ends of the budget range.

At the bottom, a planner with a $3,500 minimum charging "12%" on a $15,000 wedding is really charging 23%. Ask what the floor is before you fall for the percentage.

At the top, a $120,000 wedding at 12% is $14,400 for work a strong planner will quote at $8,000 to $10,000 flat. Percentage pricing is a market proxy for scope, not a rule. Above roughly $80,000, the flat number is the better deal for you.

Then check what the fee is charged on. A planner taking 12% of a budget that includes your rings, your honeymoon deposit and the rehearsal dinner is charging you for money you already spent. Ask for the fee to be calculated on the wedding budget only, and get that definition written into the contract rather than agreed over the phone.

Best case it is close to a wash. The typical case leaves you $500 to $1,500 short, and that is the number to accept going in. Hire a full-service planner because your job is demanding, your engagement is short, the venue is a raw space, or two families cannot agree on anything. Do not hire one expecting a positive return, and be skeptical of any planner who promises one in writing.

What a Day-of Coordinator Cannot Do

Five limits, all of them contractual rather than personal.

  • Fire or replace a vendor. They can call the florist at 9 am and ask where the centerpieces are. They cannot cancel the order, withhold payment, or find a replacement on your behalf.
  • Renegotiate anything. Every price is fixed before they arrive. If the venue charges $3 a slice for cake cutting, that is already decided.
  • Change the guaranteed guest count. Caterers lock the number 72 hours out. A coordinator working six weeks ahead can still influence it. One hired for the day cannot.
  • Build the seating chart. Six weeks is not enough time to place 140 people who all have opinions.
  • Absorb load-in alone. A venue that gives two hours for florals, rentals, the band and the cake needs a body at four doors. Ask whether the quote includes an assistant, and expect $250 to $500 when it does not.

None of that makes $1,800 a bad purchase. It makes it a narrow one. You are buying execution, not judgment, and the invoice should be priced accordingly.

Six Lines That Decide the Real Price

Both quotes stay flat until you read the terms. These six move the total by $500 to $2,000.

  1. The hours cap. "Ten hours on the wedding day" is standard and usually means 2 pm to midnight. A morning ceremony starting at 9 am eats the cap before the reception begins. Overtime runs $150 to $250 an hour, billed in full-hour blocks.
  2. Assistant, included or available. "Available" means you are paying for one. "Included" means the fee already covers it.
  3. Rehearsal attendance. Often excluded from day-of packages and billed separately at $150 to $300.
  4. Travel. Free inside 25 miles is the common line. Past it, $0.70 to $1.00 a mile, charged both directions.
  5. Contact limits. "Unlimited email" versus "up to 20 emails" changes what you get in month nine, when you finally need answers.
  6. Insurance. Ask for a certificate showing $1 million in general liability. Venues require it from caterers and increasingly from coordinators. A freelancer without coverage can leave you holding a damaged-floor bill that your own wedding policy declines to pay.

When You Need Neither

Two situations genuinely do not need the hire.

The first is a venue with a coordinator on staff and a package that covers the room, the food and the bar. Read that contract for what the in-house coordinator actually owns: usually the building, the load-in door and the F&B minimum, not your vendor timeline or your seating chart. They are not your advocate, and nobody should tell you otherwise. But when one contract supplies the room, the meal and the drinks, the gap they leave is small enough for a friend with a clipboard and a printed run-of-show.

The second is a wedding under about 60 guests in a single room with a restaurant catering it. Three vendors, one space, two hours of setup. The checklist priority calculator will hand you the sequence, and the money is better spent on the food.

How to Choose With Your Budget in Hand

  1. Count your vendors. Five or fewer, all in one room: day-of, $1,500 to $2,500. Six or more, or a raw space with rentals, power and restrooms to manage: the planner is the cheaper mistake.
  2. Check your guest count. Above 120, the timeline, the seating chart and the load-in schedule turn into a part-time job. That is where $4,000 starts looking reasonable against 200 of your evenings.
  3. Ask when they start. Anything sold as day-of that begins three weeks out is a wedding-day babysitter. Anything that begins six weeks out is a month-of package and should be priced as one.
  4. Run both through the planner cost calculator, then put the winner into the budget calculator against your ceiling. If the fee breaks the budget, cut ten guests before you cut the coordinator. At $290 a head on the average wedding, ten guests pay for the entire day-of package.
  5. Read three quotes side by side in the vendor comparison tool with the hours cap and the assistant line filled in. Two flat fees are rarely the same product.

My default for a 100-guest wedding at a full-service venue with six vendors: book the $1,800 to $2,200 day-of coordinator and keep the other $2,300. For 150 guests in a barn with rented everything, spend the $4,000 and stop pretending you will enjoy the setup. The line sits near 120 guests and six contracts. Write your number down before the first venue tour, because the tour is where budgets go to die.
