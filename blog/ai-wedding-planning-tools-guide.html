Type "plan a 120-guest wedding" into any AI wedding planning tool and it hands back a tidy table in about eight seconds. Venue $6,500. Catering $8,400. Photography $3,000. Flowers $2,400. The venue, food and bar block totals $17,300.

Then the venue emails its actual proposal. Same room, same menu, same 120 people. That block comes back at $21,573.

Nothing was added to the wedding. The missing $4,273 was in the fee stack no chatbot volunteers: a 22% service charge, sales tax applied to the service charge as well as the food, a cake-cutting fee billed per slice, and staff overtime past hour five.

That gap is the honest summary of AI wedding planning tools in 2026. They are cheap, they are useful, and they are confidently wrong about money. The average US wedding runs $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study). A tool that misses a quarter of the venue and catering block is not saving you anything, however good its checklist is.

So split the work. Let software organize the wedding. Price it with quotes.

What AI Wedding Planning Tools Cost in 2026

Pricing across the category is more uniform than the marketing implies. Five tiers, and three of them are free or close to it.

Twelve months of the most expensive app on that list is $480, or 1.4% of the average wedding. A full-service planner at 12% of a $34,200 budget is $4,104. The subscription decision is not a budget decision. Whatever you pay for software, it is not the line that breaks the plan.

The free tier is not charity. Registry and vendor platforms give away planning tools because vendors pay for placement and registries earn on gift purchases. That is a fair trade, as long as you notice which side of it you are on the next time a "recommended vendor" list appears.

One pricing habit worth keeping: pay monthly. A $39.99 app bought annually in month one costs $480 for something most couples stop opening after the invitations go out. Bought month to month for the ten months you actually use it, the same app costs $400, and you can cancel the week the seating chart is done.

Four Jobs AI Wedding Planning Tools Do Well

None of them produce a dollar figure you should trust. All four give you back hours.

Drafting the correspondence nobody wants to write. Hotel block requests, vendor inquiries, the email to your aunt about plus-ones. A decent model produces a usable first draft in fifteen seconds and you edit for tone. This is the least glamorous use and the one you will return to every week.

Turning a decision into a sequence. Give it "4:00 p.m. ceremony, no first look, 20-minute drive between hotel and venue" and it will hand back a hair-and-makeup start time, a photo window, and the dead hour you had not accounted for. The wedding timeline calculator runs the same arithmetic without the conversation.

Naming the trade-off you are avoiding. Ask what $1,200 of wedding favors costs in real terms and a good model will answer twelve guest meals at $95 a head. That reframing is worth more than any checklist it generates.

Reading a contract for charges. This is the single best use of the technology on this page, and it gets its own section below.

Where the AI Budget Comes Back Short

Two failures, and they compound.

The first is the fee stack. Hotel and restaurant catering is quoted per head, then invoiced with a service charge and sales tax applied to the total rather than to the food. Run the 120-guest example in full:

Add photography at $3,000, flowers at $2,400, a DJ at $1,500, attire at $2,400, stationery at $700, an officiant at $400 and rings at $2,000, and the wedding lands near $34,000. Which is where the national average sits. The average couple is not spending more than you are. They are paying the same fee stack.

That stack adds 30% to 40% on top of a catering subtotal in most markets, and the tax and tip calculator models it line by line so the invoice is not a surprise on the final statement.

The second failure is stale prices. Ask most models for a photographer's average rate and the answer comes back around $2,500. The 2026 figure is closer to $2,900, and the training data behind that answer was written before the price run of the last few years. The site's wedding cost inflation timeline shows how fast those numbers moved.

There is a third problem underneath both. An average is not a quote. A national average includes small-town markets at $22,000 and metro markets at $60,000, and the tool will hand you the middle without telling you that the middle is a place, not a price.

Fixed Costs and the 60-Guest Trap

A 60-guest wedding does not cost one-third of a 150-guest wedding, and any tool that multiplies a per-guest rate will tell you that it does.

Roughly $13,000 of the average wedding does not move with the guest count: photographer, DJ, attire, officiant, stationery, the venue's room fee, the rings. The variable side runs $150 to $190 per person for food, bar, rentals and favors. That puts a 60-guest wedding between $22,000 and $24,500, or about $408 a head, against $228 a head at 150 guests, which is the math behind the site's 60-guest sample budget. Scale the 150-guest average down by headcount instead and you land near $13,700, which is $10,800 under the real number.

The same mistake shows up on the invite list. A 150-name list at 65% attendance is 98 seats at $95 a head, or $9,310. Caterers want a guaranteed count 72 hours before the event, and that guarantee is a contract rather than an estimate: most will bill for the number you gave them whether or not the chairs fill. Local guests accept at about 85%, drivable guests at 65%, destination guests at 40%. Model the guarantee at 65% to 70% of the list and keep a 10% buffer for the week everyone's plans change. The guest list calculator applies those rates for you.

Vendor Emails: The Part AI Can't Fix

AI writes a perfectly polite inquiry. Photographers ignore it for the same reason they ignore every other inquiry: it reads like it went to forty of them that morning.

Four facts in the first three lines solve that. Date, venue, guest count, budget band. "We're getting married at [venue] on June 12, 2027, 120 guests, and we've set $3,000 aside for photography including the album." A vendor can answer that in one reply instead of a discovery call, and the ones who can work at that number will answer the same day.

Two rules. Never let the draft name a ceiling you have not actually decided on, because the number you type becomes the number you are quoted and vendors price to it. And send it yourself from your own inbox. Apps that automate outreach on your behalf tend to land in spam, and the reply thread is the asset you want to own.

When the quotes come back, compare them on identical lines rather than on the headline price. The vendor comparison tool lines up three quotes side by side so an $1,800 photographer with an à la carte album and a $2,600 photographer with one included stop looking like a $800 difference.

Contract Review Is the Best Use of the Tool

Paste a catering or venue contract into a general assistant with one instruction: "List every charge in this contract, including charges that only apply under certain conditions. Quote the sentence each one comes from." What comes back is usually five to eight items couples discover at the final invoice.

  • Overtime staffing. Most contracts include five hours of service. Every hour after that runs $150 to $300 across the team, billed whether the dancing continues or not.
  • Whether the service charge reaches staff. A 22% service charge can be administrative revenue. If it is not distributed as gratuity, you are tipping twice. The vendor tipping guide covers which lines actually require a separate envelope.
  • Food and beverage minimum versus room fee. Some venues credit the room fee against the minimum. Some stack it. On a $12,000 minimum with a $3,000 room fee, that clause is worth $3,000 by itself.
  • Cancellation and postponement. What percentage is refundable at 90, 60 and 30 days, and whether a rescheduled date carries the deposit forward.
  • Vendor meals. Whether the band and the photographers are fed at $30 to $60 a plate or at the full guest price, and whether those meals count toward your guarantee.

What the tool cannot do is tell you whether a clause holds up in your state. That is a lawyer's question. Three hundred dollars of a lawyer's time on a $20,000 catering contract is the cheapest insurance in the entire budget, and no subscription replaces it.

The Stack Worth Paying For

Three pieces. Anything past three is a subscription you will forget you have.

  • One organizing app, $12 to $40 a month, billed monthly. Pick it for the seating chart and the guest list, not for the AI badge. The checklist is the same list in all of them.
  • One general assistant, free tier or $20 a month. Use it for drafting and contract reading. Both jobs are short, so the free tier usually survives.
  • The calculators on this site, free. They handle the numbers the assistant gets wrong: total cost by city and guest count, tax and service charge, guest counts, and the booking priority order for the first ninety days.

That is $12 to $60 a month, and it can drop to zero in the final month when the only remaining job is showing up. What to skip: a second full planning app, because the two never stay in sync and you will update neither; annual prepayment before you have used the trial for a full week; and any design subscription that outlives the printed invitations.

Five Things to Keep Off the Chatbot

  1. Final quotes. A number in a chat window is not a price. Get it in writing, with the date, the guest count and the inclusions on the page.
  2. Alcohol quantities. "One drink per guest per hour" misses the two-drink first hour, the 15% of guests who do not drink, and the toast. Model it with the alcohol calculator instead, which splits beer, wine and liquor and accounts for the bar type.
  3. Seating charts against a real floor plan. A 60-inch round seats 8. Push it to 10 and the aisles disappear, which is how a room that "holds 140" ends up feeling cramped at 120. The venue capacity calculator works from usable square footage.
  4. Anything you are about to sign. No model reads a contract with the liability of the person signing it.
  5. Your guest list. A list is 150 to 250 names with emails, phone numbers and home addresses. Consumer chatbots retain conversations under terms almost nobody reads. Give the tool a count and an age split. Keep the names in a spreadsheet you control.

The number worth acting on: spend $200 on software for the whole engagement. That is a $20-a-month app for the ten months you will genuinely open it, or a $40 app cancelled the week the seating chart is finished. Put the other $280 of the annual price into the catering deposit, where it buys something real. Then price the wedding from written quotes, and let the tools do the part they are good at, which is remembering what you decided and telling you what you forgot.
