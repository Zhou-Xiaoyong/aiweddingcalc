The honest, judgment-free breakdown of who covers the dress, the accessories, and the alterations — plus scripts for the money talk

It's one of the first questions a bride asks after the proposal excitement fades: who pays for bridesmaid dresses? The honest answer is that there isn't one universal rule anymore. Etiquette has shifted hard over the last decade, regional habits differ, and every friendship has its own financial reality. What used to be a quiet assumption is now an open conversation — and how you handle it sets the tone for the entire bridal party experience.

This guide cuts through the awkwardness. We'll cover the modern default, what dress shopping actually costs in 2026, who covers accessories and alterations, what to do when a bridesmaid is strapped for cash, and exactly what to say so nobody feels judged.

The Short Answer: Bridesmaids Usually Pay for Their Own Dress

In the United States in 2026, the most common arrangement is still that each bridesmaid pays for her own dress. This is the modern default and what most women expect when they accept the role. Wedding planners and etiquette experts largely agree: asking someone to stand beside you is an honor, but it comes with the understood cost of the outfit.

That said, "usually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. A growing number of brides — especially those with smaller bridal parties or those who have chosen a specific, pricier gown — quietly cover the cost as a gift. And in many families, a parent (traditionally the bride's family) may fold bridesmaid attire into the broader wedding budget. The only real mistake is not talking about it until the bill arrives.

How Much Does a Bridesmaid Dress Actually Cost in 2026?

Before anyone can agree on who pays, you need a number. In 2026, the average bridesmaid dress runs about $150–$250. Budget lines start around $80, while designer and made-to-order styles can climb past $400. That range is consistent across most US retailers, and it's the figure our Bridesmaid Dress Budget Calculator uses as its baseline.

A few things push a dress toward the high end:

  • Designer labels and boutique exclusives — the single biggest cost driver.
  • Made-to-order lead times — rush cuts often add 15–30% in fees.
  • Heavy beading or structured fabrics — harder to alter and pricier to clean.
  • Convertible or mix-and-match lines — convenient, but each style is billed separately.

Remember that the sticker price is rarely the final price. Alterations, shoes, and jewelry typically add $75–$200 per woman on top. We'll break that down next.

Who Pays for Accessories & Alterations?

This is where most confusion — and most hurt feelings — happens. The widely accepted rule: if the bridesmaid buys the dress, she also budgets for the finishing touches. That means:

  • Alterations: almost always the bridesmaid's responsibility, even when the bride paid for the gown. Hemming alone often runs $30–$90.
  • Shoes: the bridesmaid's call unless the bride specifies (and pays for) a matching pair.
  • Jewelry and hairpieces: typically the bridesmaid's, though many brides gift a simple set as a thank-you.
  • Undergarments and shapewear: always the bridesmaid's.

The bride should spell this out before anyone shops. A bridesmaid who budgeted $180 for a dress can be blindsided by a $120 alterations-and-shoes surprise two months later. Clear expectations up front keep the friendship intact.

When the Bride Covers the Dresses

There are good, generous reasons a bride might pay for bridesmaid dresses herself:

  • She chose a specific, expensive style the party wouldn't have picked on their own.
  • The bridal party is small (two or three women), making the total manageable.
  • She wants a uniform look and is treating the dresses as part of the "wedding decor" budget.
  • Financial circumstances make it unkind to ask.

If you go this route, treat it as a gift rather than an obligation, and say so plainly: "I'd love us all in this color, and I'd like to cover the dresses as my thank-you." One caveat — even when the bride buys the gown, accessories and alterations usually still fall to the bridesmaid unless she explicitly says otherwise. Don't assume; clarify.

"I Can't Afford the Dress" — Handling It Gracefully

Money is the fastest way to strain a friendship, and wedding costs hit people differently. If a bridesmaid quietly struggles with the price, the kindest move is to make it easy for her to talk to you. A few graceful options:

  • Pick a flexible dress code. Choose a color and length, then let each woman find a dress in her budget — including one she already owns or can rewear.
  • Offer a budget-friendly retailer from the start so nobody feels singled out.
  • Cover her dress quietly. If you can swing it, this is the most generous fix — and you never need to announce it to the group.
  • Reconsider the roster. Sometimes the right call is a smaller party where everyone is comfortable.

Whatever you decide, keep it private and kind. Public "solution brainstorming" in the group chat can shame the person who's already uncomfortable. One direct, warm conversation beats a dozen group messages.

The Money Talk: Scripts That Don't Feel Awkward

The conversation is easier when it happens early and is framed as logistics, not charity. Try these:

  • At the ask: "I'd be honored if you'd be a bridesmaid. Dresses usually run around $200 plus shoes and alterations — just want you to know the ballpark before you say yes."
  • If you're covering it: "I've picked a style I love and I'd like to gift the dresses. You'd just handle shoes and any alterations."
  • If a friend hesitates: "No pressure at all — if cost is a concern, let's talk one-on-one. There are ways to make it work."

Notice none of these apologizes for money existing. It's a real line item in a real event. Treating it like the practical detail it is — rather than something embarrassing — makes everyone relax. Where Bridesmaid Dress Costs Fit in Your Total Wedding Budget

It helps to zoom out. The average US wedding in 2026 costs about $34,200 (The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study). Bridal-party attire is a small slice of that — often 2–4% — but it's a slice your friends feel directly in their own wallets.

For perspective, here's roughly how bridesmaid attire compares to other guest-facing line items:

  • Bridesmaid dresses + accessories (per woman): ~$250–$450
  • Full bridal party of 5: ~$1,250–$2,250
  • That's about 4–7% of a single average wedding's attire-and-decor envelope

Bridesmaid Dresses vs. Groomsmen Attire

The parallel question is whether groomsmen pay for their own suits. The answer usually mirrors the bridesmaid rule: the groomsmen typically cover their own attire, with rental often the cheaper path. A suit rental runs roughly $100–$200, versus $300+ to buy — which is why many wedding parties rent. The symmetry matters for fairness: if bridesmaids are paying, groomsmen generally should too, and vice versa.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do bridesmaids always pay for their own dresses?

In the US today, bridesmaids most often pay for their own dresses — it's the modern default. But the bride may cover the cost as a gift, especially for a small party or a pricey chosen style. The only real mistake is not discussing it early.

What if a bridesmaid can't afford the dress?

Talk early and privately. Options include a more affordable dress line, a color-only dress code that lets each woman reuse what she owns, or the bride quietly covering the cost. Keep it kind and confidential.

Who pays for alterations and accessories?

Alterations and accessories (shoes, jewelry, undergarments) are almost always the bridesmaid's responsibility, even when the bride buys the dress. The bride should make this clear before anyone shops.

Should the bride cover dresses as a gift?

It's a generous and increasingly common choice, particularly for intimate weddings or when she selected a specific gown. Treat it as a gift, and remember accessories and alterations usually still fall to the bridesmaid unless stated otherwise.
