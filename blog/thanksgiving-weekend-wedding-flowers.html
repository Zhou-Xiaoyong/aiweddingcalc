Thanksgiving weekend wedding flowers cost $629 more than the identical order on a June Saturday. Same eleven centerpieces. Same garden bouquet, four bridesmaid bouquets, six boutonnieres, one arch, one delivery.

Price it out for 88 guests and Saturday, June 6 comes back at $2,271. Saturday, November 28 comes back at $2,900. Per head, flowers move from $25.81 to $32.95.

Only $292 of that gap is a holiday fee, which is the part couples brace for. The other $337 is stems that have to fly in, a crew splitting itself across three weddings on the busiest Saturday of the fall, and an order cutoff that lands on a Tuesday.

Same Thanksgiving Flowers, Two Dates, $629 Apart

The brief is deliberately ordinary, because that is what most weddings actually order. Eleven tables: ten rounds of eight plus a head table. One low centerpiece per table, no tall stands, no floral wall. Personal flowers for a wedding party of eleven. One arch with greens.

Both numbers sit inside the national range. The average US couple spends about $34,200 on a wedding in 2026 and lands near $2,800 on flowers, per The Knot's Real Weddings data, so a Thanksgiving order at $2,900 is normal rather than gouged. It is also 8.5% of the total budget against 8.2% in June, which is the whole story in one comparison.

The 12% handling charge gets applied to the design total, not to the delivery fee alone. That is worth knowing before you negotiate, because it means every $100 of centerpiece you add costs $112 that week. What a November Centerpiece Is Made Of

A 12-stem centerpiece at $108 and a 12-stem centerpiece at $124 look the same in a photo. The difference sits in the wholesale cooler, where the same visual volume costs 26% more because four of the stems have been replaced.

Stem June, wholesale November, wholesale
3 peonies$3.40 eachswapped for 3 garden roses at $4.10
4 ranunculus$1.20 eachswapped for 3 cushion mums at $1.35
3 spray roses$1.30 eachswapped for 3 ilex berry at $1.85
2 seeded eucalyptus$0.70 eachswapped for 2 magnolia leaves at $1.15
12 stems, total$20.30$25.65

Retail floral pricing works on a multiplier, not a percentage. The usual shop formula is roughly three times the wholesale stem cost plus a fixed amount, about $47 here, for the vase, the foam, the ribbon, and the 25 minutes of design time. So June's $20.30 recipe lists at $108 and November's $25.65 recipe lists at $124.

That formula explains something couples find maddening. Take a stem out of the arrangement and the price barely moves, because the vase and the labor are fixed. Swap a $1.20 ranunculus for a $1.35 mum and you save nothing on the invoice; you have simply changed the flower. Multitiered cake logic applies to flowers too, and it is why florists talk in stem counts rather than in dollars per table.

The bigger trap is asking for peonies in late November. They exist, flown in from Holland or Chile, at $12 to $18 a stem against $4 to $6 in May. Three of them in eleven centerpieces adds $264 to $462 in wholesale cost alone, and after the 3x multiplier that is $800 to $1,400 on the quote for a flower that photographs identically to a garden rose at half the price.

Thanksgiving Weekend Wedding Flowers: The Week Everything Closes

This is the part of the holiday that has nothing to do with botany. The flower trade does not run on Thanksgiving week the way it runs the other 51 weeks, and every closure below pushes your order earlier or your price higher.

Date What the trade does What it does to your order
Thu, Nov 19Final stem counts are locked with growersLast reasonable week to change the flower list
Tue, Nov 24Wholesale order cutoff, often noon, for Friday deliveryA Wednesday add-on becomes a special order at a special price

Thu, Nov 26Wholesale markets and many retail shops closedNo substitutions, no replacements, no second truck
Fri, Nov 27Half-day markets; Christmas greenery ships at full volumeIlex and magnolia reorders get shorted first
Sat, Nov 28Three or four weddings per shop is normalYour install window is 90 minutes, not a morning
Mon, Nov 30Holiday install season opens for hotels and storefrontsStrike and pickup compete with Christmas crews

The Friday line is the one that catches people. The days after Thanksgiving are the heaviest shipping week of the year for evergreen, because every wreath, garland, and hotel lobby in the country gets built in the following ten days. Farms allocate ilex berry and magnolia to that production first. If your florist is short two bunches of berries on Friday morning, they are not being careless; they are fourth in line behind three Christmas accounts that order in August.

Saturday compounds it. The Saturday after Thanksgiving is one of the ten busiest wedding dates in the country, so a shop that handles two weddings a weekend is handling three or four, and the crews split. Ask two questions when you sign: what time is my install window, and who is on site if a table is missing a centerpiece at 4 p.m. A florist who cannot answer the second one is selling you flowers, not a wedding.

Three Flowers That Don't Survive 40 Degrees

If any part of the day happens outdoors or in a tent, the flower list changes again. Cold is not a slow problem, it is a fast one, and the failure mode is ugly rather than gradual.

Hydrangea is the first to go. Below about 40 degrees it wilts inside 90 minutes, and it does not recover in a vase once the petals have dropped; the head simply hangs and stays hung. Dahlias bruise under 45 degrees and turn translucent brown at the edges within a couple of hours. Both are summer flowers that show up in a lot of November mood boards because they photograph well in June.

What holds up is the woody and berried material. Magnolia leaves, ilex berry, seeded eucalyptus, and rose hips will look the same after four hours at 35 degrees as they did coming off the truck, which is exactly why they anchor late-fall work. Roses and cushion mums are reliable down to freezing as long as the water does not freeze. A florist who knows you are in a tent will build the arch on magnolia and skip the hydrangea without being asked. Say the word "tent" out loud, and mention the tent size you are quoting so the conversation starts from the real footprint, and read the rental numbers behind a November tent before you commit to an outdoor ceremony.

One substitution worth taking: replace three of the eleven centerpieces with candles and loose greenery. A cluster of five taper candles in rented glass runs about $38 a table against $124 for flowers, and on a 4:40 p.m. sunset the light does more for the room than the blooms do. Confirm open flame is allowed before you buy 55 candles; a lot of tent rentals require a flame permit or a battery-only rule, and that is a $180 mistake to discover in the parking lot.

Where the $2,900 Goes

Floral invoices look opaque because the lines are not itemized. Here is the same $2,900 split the way a shop actually costs it out.

  • Stems at wholesale: $835. That is 29% of what you pay. It is also the smallest line, which surprises almost everyone who assumes flowers are the expensive part.
  • Design and install labor: $1,090. Vase prep, recipe testing, conditioning, building eleven centerpieces and an arch, then installing on site for 90 minutes with two people.
  • Delivery and crew: $290. Van, fuel, tolls, a second trip if the arch does not fit, and the strike on the way home at midnight.
  • Holiday handling: $292. The 12% line, applied to the design total.
  • Overhead and shop margin: $393. Cooler electricity, insurance, the consult you had in September, and the profit that keeps the shop open in February.

Which means the negotiation you think you are having is not the one available to you. Cutting stems by $100 saves $300 at retail and guts the design. Cutting the install crew saves $290 and leaves you arranging centerpieces in a parking lot at 2 p.m. on your wedding day. The only genuinely soft line is the $292, and it is soft because it is a policy rather than a cost.

What to Cut First

Three moves, ranked by dollars saved per unit of visual damage. All three assume the eleven-table plan above.

  • Repurpose the arch onto the head table. A second arrangement for a head table of eight runs $340. Moving the arch flowers after the ceremony costs about $85 in crew time and saves the rest. You need a florist who plans the mechanics for a second life, not one who zip-ties to the structure and leaves.
  • Drop the fresh aisle markers. Six markers at $55 each is $330 for something guests see for eleven seconds and photograph from behind. Two arrangements at the ceremony entrance and a petals-only aisle gets the same photographs. Repurposing markers onto cocktail tables later is a nice idea that usually costs more in labor than it saves.
  • Convert three tables to candles and greenery. $124 becomes $38, three times over, for $258 saved. Mixing three candle tables among eight floral ones reads as a design choice on a November evening rather than as a budget cut.

Together those three land at $923, which pulls the November order down to $1,977, below the June number and comfortably inside the $1,500 to $3,500 range most couples occupy. If you only do one, do the arch. It is the largest single saving that no guest can detect.

Before you sign, get four things written into the quote: the exact install window, the holiday handling fee as a dollar figure, the delivery date and time, and the substitution policy if a stem is shorted. A floral contract without those four is a mood board with a total at the bottom.
