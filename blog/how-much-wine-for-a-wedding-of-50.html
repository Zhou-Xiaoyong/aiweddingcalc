The short answer to how much wine for a wedding of 50 guests is 16 bottles, plus 6 or 7 of sparkling for the toast. That holds for a five-hour reception where roughly seven in ten guests drink alcohol. Change the bar style or the length of the night and the number moves by four or five bottles.

The longer answer depends on how much of the drinking wine is expected to carry. A cocktail hour plus a plated dinner with two wine service points is a different animal from a backyard party where wine is one option among five. Below is the math, three bottle counts for three bar styles, and the cost side that surprises people more than the bottle count does.

Start With Drinks, Not Bottles

Nobody drinks "bottles." They drink glasses, and the bottle count is just arithmetic on the back end. The working assumption caterers use for a standing or seated reception is one drink per guest per hour, adjusted for how many guests actually drink.

For 50 guests over five hours at a 70% drinker rate, that is 175 drinks total. Fifty times five hours times 0.7. Wine's share of that 175 depends entirely on what else is on the bar:

  • Three-option bar (beer, wine, one signature cocktail): wine carries about 35% of drinks, or 61 glasses
  • Beer and wine only: wine carries about 45%, or 79 glasses
  • Wine-forward seated dinner: wine carries 60%, or 105 glasses

Then divide by five, because a standard 750ml bottle pours five 5-ounce glasses. That is where 16 bottles comes from: 79 glasses ÷ 5 = 15.8, rounded up with one spare.

One caveat specific to small weddings: plan at the top of your range, not the bottom. A 50-guest room drinks more wine per head than a 150-guest room. There is no line at the bar, the dinner sits longer, and the guest list skews toward people who are going to stand up and toast you. The two glasses of wine that a guest skips at a big dancing wedding get drunk at a small dinner.

Two rules for adjusting the table. Add one bottle for every 10 extra guests you expect to actually attend, and add two bottles if your reception runs past five hours. A six-hour reception with a late-night crowd is closer to 19 bottles on a beer-and-wine bar, not 16.

If your invitation list is 65 people, plan the bar for roughly 50 attendees, not 65. Between 75% and 85% of invited guests typically show up, and the difference between those two rates is five bottles of wine and about $70. White, Red, Rosé: The Split That Empties

At 150 guests a lopsided split barely matters. At 50 it does, because one case of wine is 12 bottles and you are only buying 16 or 21. Get the ratio wrong and you finish the night with five untouched Cabernets and no cold white left.

Start from the season and the menu, not from personal taste:

Wedding White + Rosé Red Of 16 Bottles
May–September, outdoor or warm room60%40%9–10 white/rosé · 6–7 red
October–April, candlelight and beef50%50%8 white/rosé · 8 red
Cocktail-style reception, passed apps70%30%11 white/rosé · 5 red

Rosé is the cheapest flexibility you can buy. Two bottles of dry rosé cover the guest who "doesn't really like wine" and the one who wants something cold at 4 p.m., and they cost the same as a mid-tier Sauvignon Blanc. If your menu is fish or chicken, push the white share up five points. If it's short rib or lamb, push red up five.

Pick two whites and two reds, maximum. A 50-guest wedding with six open varietals means four bottles that get opened, half-poured and dumped. Curate to a crisp white, a rounder white or rosé, a light red, and a fuller red. That is a bar, not a restaurant list.

Sparkling for the Toast

A toast pour is not a wine pour. It's about 4 ounces, which fills eight flutes from one 750ml bottle. For 50 guests where roughly 45 take a glass, that is 5.6 bottles, so buy 6 or 7 and keep one cold as insurance.

Do not fold sparkling into your wine count. It's served at a single moment, it's poured all at once, and the leftover behaves differently from still wine.

Prosecco and Cava run $10–$15 a bottle; true Champagne starts around $40 and climbs fast. In a flute, at a toast, with 50 people clapping, almost nobody can tell the difference. Put the saved $180 into the two dinner wines instead, where guests actually sit with the glass for 45 minutes.

If you're pouring sparkling through cocktail hour as well, double up to 10–12 bottles. That turns a $75 line item into a $150 one, which is worth knowing before you promise a Champagne welcome.

The 4-Ounce Problem

Here is the number that makes wine ordering feel unreliable: the same 16 bottles pour a different number of glasses depending on who is holding the bottle.

Sixteen bottles hold about 406 ounces. Poured at 5 ounces, that's 81 glasses. Poured at 4 ounces, it's 101 glasses. Same purchase, 25% more servings, no extra money.

Venues know this, which is why they pour 4 ounces into a 6-ounce glass with room to spare. It looks generous, it stretches the bottle, and it keeps guests from getting wine-drunk before dinner. If you're self-pouring from bottles on the tables, expect the opposite: guests pour 6 or 7 ounces without thinking, and your 16 bottles turn into about 60 servings instead of 80.

The practical fix is a wine key and a pour spout, not a speech. Slow-pour spouts cost a few dollars each and cut the average self-pour by an ounce. On a 50-guest wedding that is the difference between running out at 9:45 and finishing the night with three returnable bottles.

What the Wine Actually Costs

For context, the average US wedding runs about $34,200 in 2026 (The Knot Real Weddings Study), and the bar usually claims 8–12% of the total. Wine is roughly a third of a self-supplied bar bill.

Compare that to a venue charging $9–$14 a glass. At five glasses a bottle, that's $45–$70 per bottle equivalent, or $1,000–$1,600 for the same 23 bottles. Self-supplying doesn't just save a little; it changes the budget category.

Buy by the case where the math works. Twelve-bottle cases usually earn a 10–15% discount, and many shops extend a smaller discount to six-bottle mixed cases. At 16 bottles you're buying one case plus four singles, so ask for a mixed case at the case price rather than assuming the discount applies. It often does, and it's worth $25–$40 for one question at the register.

Corkage: Where the Savings Die

Before you buy 23 bottles, get one number in writing: the corkage fee. It typically runs $10–$25 per bottle, and at the top of that range it can cost more than the wine.

Twenty-three bottles at $25 corkage is $575. Your wine cost $276. You would have paid the venue less to just pour its own list.

The break-even is easy to run. Venue wine lists typically price at 2.5 to 3 times retail, so a $12 bottle sells for $30–$36. Add $15 corkage to that $12 bottle and you're at $27, ahead by $3–$9. Add $25 corkage and you're at $37, behind by a dollar or two on every bottle.

In short: corkage punishes cheap wine and rewards good wine. Break-even corkage is roughly 1.5 to 2 times the retail price of the bottle, so a $12 bottle stops working past $18–$24 corkage, while an $18 bottle still clears at $25. If your venue charges $25 and you were planning $10 supermarket wine, drink their list instead.

Three more things to confirm in writing, not on a call:

  • Who holds the liquor license. Some states require a licensed server for any alcohol service, and a few venues require their own staff to open and pour anything you bring. That can add a labor charge per hour.
  • Whether corkage is charged per bottle opened or per bottle brought. The difference on 23 bottles at $15 is $345, so ask exactly.
  • Whether the fee replaces or stacks with a service charge. A 20% service charge on the corkage line is a real line item, and it also gets sales tax in most states.

Run the whole stack through the tax and tip calculator before you decide. Corkage plus service charge plus tax on a $12 bottle can quietly reach the venue's list price, which is the outcome that makes couples feel cheated without anyone having done anything wrong.

Chilling, Opening & Serving Order

Wine bought correctly can still be served badly. Four things decide whether 50 guests enjoy it.

Temperature. Sparkling at 45°F, whites at 45–50°F, reds at 60–65°F. A banquet room sits at 70–72°F, so a red left on the table all evening reads hot and alcoholic and guests leave half the glass. Chill the reds for 20 minutes before service, then let them warm in the glass. It is the single cheapest upgrade to your wine program.

Chill in waves. You need about 8–10 bottles cold at any moment for 50 guests, not all 23. Ice down whites and sparkling in tubs, keep reds upright in the shade, and restock from the cases as the night moves. Wine that sits in ice for four hours gets waterlogged labels and, if the cork is compromised, a diluted bottle.

Open four or five at a time. Fifty guests drink about one bottle every eight minutes during dinner service. Opening all 16 at 6 p.m. means a dozen bottles oxidizing on a table by 8. Open in small batches, and open reds 30 minutes ahead if you're not decanting.

Put two bottles on each table. For 50 guests you'll have six or seven tables. Self-serve wine on the tables is what keeps the bar line short enough that one bartender can handle 50 people drinking beer, wine, and one signature cocktail. The working ratio is one bartender per 50 guests once cocktails are on the menu, and one per 75 if it's beer and wine only.

Leftovers are fine, within limits. Recorked red and white hold for 3–5 days in the fridge; sparkling is flat by the next morning. That's the argument for opening the Prosecco last, right before the toast, and not a minute earlier.

Get Your Own Bottle Count

Fifty guests is a starting point, not your wedding. A Sunday brunch with 60% drinkers and a 3-hour reception can land 40% below these numbers, and a Saturday night with a heavy-drinking crowd can run 30% above. Adjust the drinker percentage, the hours, and the bar type, and let the tool do the division.

Then take the bottle count to your caterer alongside the food quote. Wine and food pricing interact, and a licensed caterer often has a wholesale wine program that beats retail once you factor in corkage.
